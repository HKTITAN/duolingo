# The anomaly is the story

## Concept

An aggregate ranking tells the reader what they already believed and gives nobody a reason to pass it on. An anomaly does the opposite: it creates a question in the reader's head, and the answer is what they repeat. The move is a two-step. Find the cells that contradict the obvious pattern, then explain each one using a source you do not own — census figures, migration statistics, trade ties, historical record, a public index. The outside source is what converts a number only you can produce into a claim about the world, which is the thing a journalist can actually use and the thing that makes the finding feel like knowledge rather than a dashboard readout.

## What Duolingo does

- The 2016 country report mapped the top studied language in **all 194 countries** from **three months of activity across 120M users and 19 languages**, then chased every oddity in the map rather than leading on the headline that **English was studied by 53% of all users and by over half of users in 94 countries** (Spanish 17%, French 11%, German 7%).
- Each exception gets an externally sourced explanation: **Swedish tops the chart in Sweden at 27%** — explained by refugee arrivals, with the outside fact that one in six Swedish residents was foreign-born in 2015; **English is second in the US at 21%** — explained with the figure that over 25 million people there speak English less than "very well"; **Italian is second in Argentina at 11%** — explained by up to 63% of the population having Italian descent; **German tops Bosnia and Namibia** — explained by guest-worker history and German rule until 1990. Norwegian is 18% in Norway, Portuguese 21% in Mozambique and 12% in Angola.
- The same post is candid that some of its own anomalies may be supply artifacts rather than real ones: for nearly every country where English was not top-two, no English course existed from that source language, and French was available from two more source languages than Spanish. An anomaly you created is not an anomaly.
  Source: blog.duolingo.com/which-countries-study-which-languages-and-what-can-we-learn-from-it (Duolingo blog, 2016-05-05; accessed 2026-09-22)
- Later editions keep the pattern, tying odd movements to events readers already care about: Russian displaced by Portuguese amid the war in Ukraine, Korean overtaking Italian, and **Swedish losing the top spot in Sweden for the first time on record** — an anomaly reported as the reversal of the earlier anomaly, which only a maintained series can do.
  Source: blog.duolingo.com/2023-duolingo-language-report (Duolingo blog, 2023-12-04; accessed 2026-09-22)
- Tension: an outside source explains, it does not prove. Every one of these is a plausible correlation dressed carefully as a suggestion, and the discipline is to keep it dressed that way even when the story is good.

## The transferable pattern

Treat the ranking as the scaffolding and the exceptions as the content.

1. **Sort for surprise, not for size.** After building the table, list the cells that violate the obvious pattern, and rank those by how odd they are.
2. **Explain each one with a source you do not control.** Public statistics, historical record, an official register, a third-party index. If you cannot find one, say the anomaly is unexplained rather than inventing a mechanism.
3. **Check the boring explanation first.** Before publishing an anomaly, rule out your own pricing, availability, launch calendar and marketing spend in that segment. Most surprising cells are self-inflicted.
4. **Write the explanation as a suggestion.** "Likely reflects" and "consistent with" are not hedging; they are accuracy, and they are what keeps the finding standing when somebody checks it.
5. **Watch for the anomaly reversing.** An oddity that flips in a later edition is a better story than the original, and it is only available to a publisher who kept the series running.

## Apply to your product

- In your last segment table, which three cells did somebody internally describe as "weird" — and did any of that reach the published version?
- For each of those, what public dataset could you check it against, and what would it take to rule out your own pricing or availability as the cause?
- Which anomaly in your data have you been explaining internally with a story nobody has tested?

# Friction Is Symmetric

## Concept

Users do not choose by absolute effort, they choose by relative effort among whatever is available in the moment. That makes friction symmetric — one second removed from your path and one second added to the competitor's path have the same effect on the comparison. Product teams almost always work only one side, because the other side is not theirs to build. But the competing behaviour is usually one the user already wishes they did less of, which means they will happily help you add the friction if you tell them how.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/adhd-study-tricks (Duolingo blog, 2022-03-15; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo's advice to learners whose main competitor is their own phone is explicitly about handicapping the alternative — use Focus Mode, uninstall rival apps, or put the phone in a drawer and use Duolingo on desktop instead.
- The stated principle — "Putting obstacles between yourself and the distractions can make your higher priorities more appealing." Note the mechanism claimed is that the *priority becomes more appealing*, without the priority changing at all. Only the comparison moved.
- The tactics are handed to the user to perform, not built into the product. Duolingo cannot add friction to a competitor; the user can, and will.
- Tension the post names directly — "Phones are so distracting. But also, Duolingo is on your phone." The advice cuts against Duolingo's own primary distribution channel. Following it to the letter moves the user onto desktop, a surface with worse notification reach and worse session frequency.

## The transferable pattern

Treat the competing behaviour as part of your design surface even though you do not own it.

1. **Name the actual competitor.** Not the market-map competitor — the thing your user does instead, in that exact slot. It is usually a feed, a chat client, or nothing at all.
2. **Ask which side is cheaper this quarter.** Shaving a step off your own flow may be a month of engineering; telling users one setting change may be a sentence of copy.
3. **Hand the user the lever.** Focus modes, app limits, physical separation, blocked hours. They are already ambivalent about the competitor, which is why this reads as help rather than as an attack.
4. **Check whether the friction lands on you too.** If the competitor lives on the same device, channel or surface you depend on, the tactic can take your own reach down with it. Say so out loud and measure the net.
5. **Do not add friction inside your own product to make usage feel earned.** That is a different mechanic with a different risk profile, and it is not what this pattern claims.

## Apply to your product

- What specifically does your user do instead of you, in the same five minutes — and could you make that thing one step harder rather than making yourself one step easier?
- Is there a setting on the user's device or in their workflow that you could simply tell them to change?
- If your advice moves users off the channel you reach them on, does the net still come out positive?

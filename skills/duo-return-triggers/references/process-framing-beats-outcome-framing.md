# Process Framing Beats Outcome Framing

## Concept

Name the behaviour you want people to build, not the prize they will get for building it. A goal is a distant outcome the user has to believe in before the copy does any work. A process is something they can start today, which is a smaller and more credible commitment. The difference shows up most on a bad day — process framing survives one, because doing the thing badly is still doing the thing, while goal framing turns a bad day into evidence that the goal is slipping away, which is the moment most people quit.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/putting-in-work-the-habit-of-language-learning (Duolingo blog, 2021-01-08; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo tested habit language systematically across three separate surfaces rather than once — push notifications, the notification opt-in screen, and the screen shown right after a first lesson.
- On the **notification opt-in screen**, habit-framed copy **lifted opt-ins by 5%**.
- On the **post-first-lesson screen**, "Reach a 7 day streak to start building a daily habit" replaced "This starts your streak! Keep it going tomorrow!" — the habit-framed version **brought learners back in greater numbers**.
- In **push**, the habit-framed January 2020 message beat the resolution-framed control, "Reach your goals! Keep your resolution going with a lesson." January is precisely when goal framing should have the home advantage, and it still lost.
- Note what the winning version does mechanically — it names a **specific, bounded, near-term behaviour** (seven days) and says what that behaviour is *for* (a daily habit). It is not vaguer than the control; it is more concrete about the process and less concrete about the payoff.

## The transferable pattern

When you are asking for a recurring behaviour, write the ask about the behaviour.

- **Name the action and its cadence**, not the end state. "Log one thing a day this week" over "hit your quarterly target."
- **Keep the horizon short enough to be believed.** A week is a commitment; a quarter is a wish.
- **Say what the repetition builds.** Process framing without a reason is just nagging; the phrase "to start building a daily habit" is doing real work.
- **Check the failure branch.** Read your copy as someone who just missed a day. If it now reads as an accusation or an obituary for their goal, it is outcome framing.
- **Do not assume it generalizes across surfaces.** The framing was won on three separate surfaces with three separate experiments, not one rollout.

## Apply to your product

- Take your three highest-traffic pieces of retention copy. How many name a reward or a target rather than a behaviour and a cadence?
- What is the smallest repeated action in your product that someone could commit to for seven days?
- Reread your best-performing nudge as a user who has just failed twice. Does it still give them a way back in?

# Optimize for the Side Nobody Has Served

## Concept

Plenty of markets have two sides — one that buys and one that has to go through the thing. The incumbent's customer is the buyer, so the incumbent optimizes for buyer trust and treats the other side's time, money and stress as externalities. Those externalities are a large pool of unclaimed value, and because the incumbent has no incentive to claim it, you can hold that ground for years. Once enough of the enduring side prefers you, the buying side follows them.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-we-improved-the-duolingo-english-test-in-2021 (Duolingo blog, 2021-12-22; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo named the neglected side explicitly and built around it — an internal concept called **TTX**, the test taker experience — covering free readiness resources, a lower price, an intuitive interface, shorter testing time, fast score turnaround, and **on-demand delivery 24 hours a day**.
- The gap it exploited was priced in dollars and hours. **$65 against $200+** for the established alternative; the test was taken in **over 12,000 cities in 2021**. Institutional acceptance was then pushed behind that individual preference, not ahead of it.
- **Tension.** Expanding access raised the security burden directly. The same post that celebrates the reach also describes hiring attackers to break the test — cheap remote access is also cheap access for cheaters, and the cost of serving the neglected side lands on the side that was already being served.
- The largest cost the incumbent never priced was outside their product boundary entirely. Source: blog.duolingo.com/future-of-digital-testing (Duolingo blog, 2023-06-06; accessed 2026-09-22) — the test replaced the venue rather than improving it: **at home, on demand, in under an hour**, instead of booking a fixed date and travelling to a crowded room where, as one student describes it, everyone writing, erasing and fidgeting is especially distracting during speaking sections.
- The access cost was real money for real people — traditional exam fees **can exceed a month's salary** in some parts of the world, and institutions accept the alternative partly because it reaches people who live far from centres, have mobility issues, or cannot afford the fee.

## The transferable pattern

Find the side of your market that endures your category rather than buying it — the candidate being interviewed, the patient filling the form, the contractor onboarding to a client's system, the employee whose manager bought the tool. Write down what their hour actually costs them, including the parts outside anyone's product boundary: travel, scheduling, waiting, re-entry of data they already gave someone.

Then treat that list as your feature roadmap and sequence adoption in that order. Win the enduring side first, and let their preference be the argument you take to the buying side. It is a stronger argument than a feature comparison, because the buyer cannot easily dismiss the experience of the people they are responsible for.

Two things to plan for. Deleting a venue or a gate is almost always a bigger move than improving what happens inside it, because it cuts cost, improves experience and expands the addressable population at the same time — three wins one in-venue improvement cannot produce. And whatever gate you delete was doing some work; budget explicitly for replacing that work, because the abuse it prevented will arrive at your door instead.

## Apply to your product

- Who in your market has no choice about using your category, and who signs the cheque? If those are different people, what has never been fixed for the first group?
- Which cost of using your product falls outside your product boundary — travel, waiting, scheduling, re-keying — and what would deleting it rather than shrinking it look like?
- What work is the gate you want to remove quietly doing, and what will you have to build to replace it?

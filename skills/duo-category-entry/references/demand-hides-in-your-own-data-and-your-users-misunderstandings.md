# Demand Hides in Your Own Data and Your Users' Misunderstandings

## Concept

Competitive analysis tells you what exists. Your own data tells you what people want and cannot get. Stated interest from your existing population is pre-qualified — these people already chose you, so the only open question is the missing capability, not acquisition. And a misconception that keeps recurring is not individual ignorance; it is evidence of a systematic absence in what is available, which means everyone who shares the assumption is an addressable audience.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/2021-duolingo-language-report (Duolingo blog, 2021-12-06; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo surveyed its own learners and found that **94% of those whose family language is endangered, indigenous or otherwise under-studied said they would be interested or very interested in learning it** — then announced Zulu and Xhosa courses on the strength of it.
- Supporting motivation data from the same report: in a consumer survey, **65% of all respondents named learning about a new culture as their top reason** to study a language, ahead of every other motivator, and **70% of those who started during the pandemic** said their learning related to family heritage, ancestry or culture. Welsh remained among the UK's fastest-growing languages **six years after launch**.
- **Tension.** Duolingo publicly asked the uncomfortable follow-up — whether Spanish and French interest in South Africa would decline once local languages were available. A new line drawn from your own population may cannibalize the old one, and stated interest is not a purchase.
- Absence of tooling is not absence of demand. Duolingo shipped Haitian Creole in February 2022 — a language preserved largely through spoken word with little institutionalized teaching material, where **over 90% of Haiti's population speaks only Creole** — and it immediately served diaspora and adoptee learners who had no other route back to it. Source: blog.duolingo.com/haitian-creole-learner-stories (Duolingo blog, 2022-09-12; accessed 2026-09-22)
- A recurring wrong question works the same way. Tour guide Kévi Donat created his Paris noir tour in 2011 after an American tourist at the Panthéon looked at a photograph of Aimé Césaire and asked "Who's this African-American man?" — the repeated misunderstanding convinced him the gap was in what tours existed, not in that visitor. Duolingo later built a podcast episode on top of the tour, published in 2021. Source: blog.duolingo.com/black-history-french-podcast (Duolingo blog, 2021-07-14; accessed 2026-09-22)

## The transferable pattern

Three demand signals you already own and probably do not read.

1. **Stated interest at scale from current users.** Ask your own population what they want that you do not offer, and report it as a percentage of a named segment rather than as anecdotes. It predicts where the market is going before the market reports it — but treat it as a ranking signal, because saying yes to a survey costs nothing.
2. **Categories served informally.** Where a need was historically met by people rather than by systems — a relative, a mentor, a local expert — there is no market signal at all, because informal transmission leaves no trace. The demand only becomes visible when the informal channel breaks. Conventional analysis reads these categories as empty; they are unserved, which is the opposite.
3. **Recurring misconceptions.** When the same wrong assumption arrives from unconnected users, the gap it reveals is usually worth building for rather than correcting one person at a time. The frequency of the mistake is a free estimate of the audience size.

Before committing, force the cannibalization question out loud — if the new line draws from the same population as the old, some of its demand is being moved rather than created, and you should know which part.

## Apply to your product

- What would happen if you asked your current users, this quarter, what adjacent thing they want that you do not sell — and reported the answer as a percentage of a defined segment?
- Which part of your users' workflow is still handled by a person rather than a system, because no tool ever existed for it?
- What do support tickets get wrong repeatedly? Is that a documentation failure or a missing product?

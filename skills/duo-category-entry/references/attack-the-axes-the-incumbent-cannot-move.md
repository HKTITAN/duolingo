# Attack the Axes the Incumbent Cannot Move

## Concept

Where the binding constraint on demand is access rather than quality, competing on quality inside the incumbent's format leaves the actual constraint untouched. Every access barrier you remove converts a non-consumer into a user, and removing several at once compounds into a different category rather than a cheaper version of the same one. The axes worth attacking are the ones the incumbent's cost structure or business model physically prevents them from following you on.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/antony-kunnan-interview (Duolingo blog, 2022-08-08; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The Duolingo English Test launched in **2016** and moved four axes simultaneously — **$49** against incumbents costing hundreds; **one hour** against several; **taken at home** against a test centre; **computer-adaptive** against linear. A leading assessment researcher's account of first encountering it enumerates exactly those four as anomalous at once.
- Access was treated as product surface, not charity. Duolingo remains the only testing organization offering international student fee waivers for high-stakes admissions tests, with **over 10,000 fee waivers issued** through its access program.
- **Tension.** Accessibility was praised by critics years before validity was. The same researcher recommended competitors adopt the test's practices while still declining to endorse it for admissions. Format innovation buys attention immediately; the evidence that justifies it is a separate, slower project.
- Sizing by exclusion, not by market share. Duolingo entered music by teaching general music literacy — reading and identifying notes, rhythm, playing familiar songs — on an **on-screen keyboard requiring no instrument**, explicitly rejecting competitors' approach of training performance on a specific instrument. The stated sizing was **3.6 million U.S. students lacking access to music education**, against **private lessons costing up to $400 per session**. Source: blog.duolingo.com/music-course (Duolingo blog, 2023-10-11; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **Tension.** Breadth cost depth. The course cannot serve someone who wants to actually play an instrument well, and at launch every lesson was piano-keyboard based.

## The transferable pattern

List the axes your category competes on, then mark each one with what it would cost the incumbent to move it. Price they can cut once. Duration, place of use, prerequisite equipment, and turnaround are usually structural — bound to a venue, a workforce, a certification body, or a per-seat cost they cannot delete without dismantling the business. Those are the axes to attack, and attacking three or four together is what makes you a new category instead of a discount.

Size the opportunity by who is currently **priced out**, not by who is currently served. People locked out by cost or prerequisites are invisible in market research because they are nobody's customer. Removing the prerequisite does not split the existing market; it creates a larger one beside it.

Two costs to accept going in. Breadth forfeits the advanced user — the person who wants mastery will stay with the specialist, and pretending otherwise produces an offering that serves neither. And the experts who validate your category will praise your accessibility long before they endorse your quality, so budget for the slow evidence project separately from the launch.

## Apply to your product

- Which axis in your category is structurally frozen for the leader — what in their cost base or distribution makes it impossible to move, not merely unattractive?
- How many people are excluded from your category entirely by price or by a prerequisite? Is that number bigger than the market you were planning to compete for?
- Which respected voice in your space will call you accessible before they call you good, and what evidence would change the second verdict?

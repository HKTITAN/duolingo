# Repeated Inbound Requests Are the Spec

## Concept

A new product line normally carries three unknowns at once. Does anyone want this? Can we reach the people who do? Will they trust us to deliver it? Demand that arrives unprompted from your existing users answers all three in a single stroke — the askers exist, you already have a channel to them, and they are asking *you* specifically. What is left is the ordinary risk of execution, which is the only one you are actually good at managing.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/duolingos-most-popular-course-turned-high-stakes-test (Duolingo blog, 2021-07-21; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Learners who had progressed through Duolingo's English courses repeatedly asked whether there was a way to certify their proficiency as accessibly as they had learned it. The ask named the adjacency precisely — same subject, next step, same expectation of price and convenience.
- That inbound demand, combined with the founders' own experience of expensive certification as immigrants, became the Duolingo English Test.
- The existing capability was the differentiated input, not a coincidence. Duolingo had already taught English to millions of learners from **over 20 first languages**, which is exactly the population and the data a proficiency test needs.
- **Tension.** The askers are your most engaged users, so they under-represent the doubts of everyone else. Duolingo's existing audience trusted the brand instantly; the institutions that had to accept the resulting scores did not, and winning them was a separate multi-year project with its own evidence requirements — see [[optimize-for-the-side-nobody-has-served]].

## The transferable pattern

Keep a running tally of what users ask you for that you do not sell. Not a wishlist of features inside the current product — requests that sit just outside its boundary. The valuable ones share three properties.

1. **Repetition over time** from people who are not connected to each other. One loud customer is an account issue; the same request from strangers is a market.
2. **Adjacency to a capability you already have.** The reason the ask lands on you is that you already do the hard part. If the request would require you to build something unrelated from scratch, the trust you inherit is worth much less than it looks.
3. **A named end-state.** "Can I prove it?", "Can I share this with my manager?", "Can I get this in a format my auditor accepts?" — these describe a deliverable, which means the spec is already half written.

Two cautions. Your askers are your most engaged segment, so their demand is real but their tolerance is not representative. And if the new line has a second audience that must accept or approve the output, inbound demand from users buys you nothing with them; that is a separate campaign, budgeted separately.

## Apply to your product

- What have users asked you for more than five times in the last year that you do not offer? Is anyone writing it down?
- For the top one — which part of it would you be building from a position of existing strength, and which part from zero?
- Does the thing they are asking for have to be accepted by someone other than them? If so, who, and what would convince that person?

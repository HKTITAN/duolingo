# Segment by stated motive, not demographics

## Concept

Demographics tell you who a user is. Motive tells you what the product is *for* them, and only the second one predicts whether they are still here in a year. Two users with identical age, country, plan tier and device can sit on opposite retention curves because one arrived to get a thing done and the other arrived to stay connected to somebody. If you ask for the reason at signup and then never cut a metric by it, you have collected the most predictive field you own and thrown it away.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/which-country-studies-english-the-most (Duolingo blog, 2023-07-10; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo ranked English learners by average new lessons per day against their self-reported reason for joining. The order, highest activity to lowest: **connect with people, prepare for travel, spend time productively, boost my career, support my education, just for fun**.
- The sample: learners in **Argentina, Brazil, Germany, India and the UK** who joined in **2023**, with average new lessons per day measured over **March–April 2023**. India ranked **#1** on average daily lessons, ahead of Germany, the UK, Brazil and Argentina.
- Scale context for the cut: English was the **#1 studied language in 119 countries in 2022**, so this is a motive split inside one enormous, otherwise undifferentiated population.
- Duolingo points out that travel is itself a form of social connection — which puts **both** of the top two slots on the relational side of the line, and the career and coursework motives below them.
- The bottom-ranked motive is the externally imposed one. Duolingo's reading is that doing it because someone requires it may simply be less motivating than doing it for a personal reason.
- Tension: Duolingo offers a competing explanation for the school cohort ranking low — those users may be doing the same work somewhere else, so the ranking partly measures **substitution**, not only motivation strength. A low-activity motive cohort is a question to investigate, not a verdict to design around.

## The transferable pattern

- **Instrumental motive is spent by its own outcome.** If the user is here to reach a result, every session is a cost they are trying to minimise, and the moment the result arrives — or the deadline slips — the daily pull stops. Their engagement is a countdown.
- **Relational motive is partly paid by the activity itself.** Every interaction with another person renews the reason rather than drawing it down, so it regenerates instead of depleting.
- **Obligation is the weakest fuel in the set.** Use that someone else requires is borrowed motivation. It ends when the requirement ends, and it produces the smallest voluntary effort while it lasts.
- **So ask the question at signup, in the user's words, and store it as a first-class dimension.** One dropdown, four to six options, and then every retention chart gets a cut by it.
- **Then design the hook around the relational reason even when your product is nominally a utility.** A workflow tool with a shared-with-a-colleague path has a renewable reason to exist; the same tool used alone has a countdown.
- **Read a low-activity cohort twice.** Ask whether they are unmotivated or simply doing the same job in another tool before you conclude the motive is weak.

## Apply to your product

- Do you capture a stated reason at signup, and is any retention dashboard in your company currently cut by it?
- Which of your motives is satisfied by its own outcome, and what happens to those users the week after they get what they came for?
- Where in your product could two users encounter each other, and would that turn a countdown motive into a renewable one?

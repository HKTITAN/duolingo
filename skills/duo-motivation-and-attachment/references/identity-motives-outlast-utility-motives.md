# Identity motives outlast utility motives

## Concept

Some reasons expire and some do not. A goal attached to a task — a certification, a trip, a job requirement — has a completion condition built into it, and the daily pull dies at that condition whether the user succeeded or gave up. A goal attached to who the user is, or who their family is, has no completion condition at all and no substitute product that can satisfy it instead. This is why your smallest and least commercially obvious segments are frequently your most committed ones, and why they are almost never the segments your roadmap is written for.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/boomers-vs-gen-z-duolingo-streaks (Duolingo blog, 2023-01-19; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo's **most daily-committed** learners were not in its biggest courses. They were in **Scottish Gaelic, Welsh and Yiddish** — courses its own earlier research showed are chosen for **family and culture** reasons — ranking ahead of the vastly larger Spanish and French cohorts, which are largely studied for school and work.
- The Nordic courses show the same shape at the extreme: **Norwegian, Swedish and Danish each have more than 70% of learners on unbroken streaks**.
- The motive split behind it is measurable, not assumed: **34% of U.S. Yiddish learners name culture as their primary reason — over three times the rate for English, Spanish or French — and 54% name family or culture combined**.
- The commercially obvious cut would have been course size. Course size and course commitment point in opposite directions here, and a revenue-weighted view of the same data would have shown only Spanish and French.

## The transferable pattern

- **Sort your motives by whether they can be completed.** A motive with a finish line generates effort that decays toward that line. A motive tied to identity, family or belonging has no finish line, so its daily pull is flat rather than decaying.
- **Check substitutability too.** A user pursuing a practical outcome can be served by a competitor, a workaround, or by the need evaporating. A user for whom the activity *is* the point has nowhere else to take it.
- **Expect an inverse relationship between segment size and segment commitment.** The mass segment is usually mass because the need is generic, and generic needs are the ones with deadlines attached.
- **Do not let cohort size decide what you build.** Rank segments by retained users and by revenue per retained user, not by signups, or the durable minority never appears on the chart.
- **Name the identity back to the user.** A segment that came for a reason about who they are will respond to copy, surfaces and community that reflect that reason — and will not respond to efficiency claims aimed at the deadline-driven majority.
- Tension worth stating in the room: the small committed segment can absolutely be too small to fund. The mistake is not choosing the big segment — it is choosing it without knowing that the small one retains three times better.

## Apply to your product

- Which of your segments has no completion condition — nothing that, once achieved, makes the product unnecessary?
- Rank your segments by 12-month retention rather than by signups. Which ones move up, and has anyone on your team seen that list?
- Is there a cohort you deprioritised for being small that is, per user, the most committed group you have?

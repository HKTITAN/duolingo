# The growth cohort is not the retention cohort

## Concept

The segment driving your growth chart and the segment driving your long-term revenue are usually two different groups of people, and aggregate metrics are structurally incapable of showing you this. The growing segment dominates the numerator of everything that moves week to week — signups, new-feature adoption, social chatter — while the durable segment quietly dominates the denominator of lifetime value. Because the growth segment is the one generating noise, it is the one your roadmap hears. Novelty-seeking and persistence are simply different traits, and there is no reason to expect them in the same person.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/2023-duolingo-language-report (Duolingo blog, 2023-12-04; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo reports the split directly: **Gen Z drives the interest in newer and less commonly studied courses, while Boomers hold the longest streaks of any generation.** One cohort supplies the growth story; another supplies the durability.
- The growth side is heavily concentrated in one age band. **Over half of Ukrainian learners are Gen Z. 86% of Japanese and 76% of Chinese learners are under 30. 72% of new Portuguese learners are under 30.** Any course-level growth chart is, effectively, a chart of what one cohort is currently curious about.
- The durability side runs the other way, and it persists through failure: **even after a break, older learners are less likely than Gen Z to lose a newly restarted streak.** Persistence is not just higher in that cohort, it is more resilient to interruption.
- The practical consequence is that a single "engagement is up" number mixes a curiosity signal and a commitment signal that are moving for unrelated reasons, and gives you no way to tell which one moved.

## The transferable pattern

- **Report acquisition and retention as two segmented tables, never as one trend line.** For each segment: share of new users, and 6- or 12-month retention. The interesting rows are the ones where those two ranks disagree.
- **Assume the traits are inversely distributed until your data says otherwise.** The appetite for trying something new and the willingness to keep doing it are different dispositions. The people generating your adoption curve are selected for the first one.
- **Label every feature with which cohort it is for.** A feature aimed at novelty — a new surface, a new category, a launch moment — should be evaluated on acquisition and should not be defended with a retention argument it was never going to win.
- **Watch for recovery asymmetry.** Measure what happens after a lapse by segment, not just in aggregate. A cohort that restarts and sticks is worth more than a cohort with the same raw return rate that restarts and lapses again.
- **Beware of whose feedback you are hearing.** New users are the ones filling out the onboarding survey and posting about the product. The durable segment stopped commenting years ago, which is exactly why they are still here.
- Tension: this does not mean serve the durable cohort and ignore the growing one. It means stop pretending one set of numbers tells you about both, and make the tradeoff deliberately rather than by default.

## Apply to your product

- Which segment supplies most of your new users this quarter, and which segment supplies most of your revenue from users acquired two years ago? Are they the same?
- Take your last three shipped features. Which cohort was each one actually for, and was it judged on that cohort's metric?
- After a lapse and a restart, does every segment stick at the same rate — or do you not measure restart survival at all?

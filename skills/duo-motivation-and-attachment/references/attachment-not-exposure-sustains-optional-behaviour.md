# Attachment, not exposure, sustains optional behaviour

## Concept

For a behaviour nobody is required to perform, availability is not the constraint — wanting is. More surfaces, more reminders and more impressions raise the number of opportunities to act without changing the probability of acting, because the user was never blocked by not knowing the product existed. Attachment does change it: it converts the behaviour from a cost the user pays into an identity the user holds, and an identity survives the days when motivation is low. This is also the answer when the core task is long and genuinely hard. You do not make the task easier; you make the world around it worth being in.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/tips-for-teaching-kids-your-mother-language (Duolingo blog, 2025-02-18; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo's advice for parents raising a child in a heritage language leads with the thesis rather than a tactic: **"The most important thing is helping children build an emotional bond with your language"** — and explicitly, **"the key isn't just exposure."**
- All **five** of its tactics are bond-builders, not volume plays: one caregiver using it consistently, other speakers via visits and video calls, a home library, nightly lullabies, and shared cultural rituals like cooking a childhood dish.
- Tension Duolingo states itself: you cannot engineer the input side. **"Creating a perfect balance of exposure between languages is practically impossible"**, and elsewhere Duolingo calls balanced bilinguals **"a myth."** Its fallback is that partial, inconsistent effort still compounds — **"even some exposure can go a long way."**

Source: blog.duolingo.com/building-character (Duolingo blog, 2020-11-10; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The character cast was designed explicitly **"to make it easier for our learners to spend time within our product,"** modelled on the designer's experience of wandering Zelda's open world long after finishing the game — attachment that outlives the objective.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/why-do-we-have-traditions (Duolingo blog, 2023-11-15; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The belonging mechanism is Benedict Anderson's **imagined community**: a group far too large for anyone to know all its members still produces real kinship, fuelled by markers held in common. Duolingo applies it to itself — hearing the correct-answer sound in public makes you look around for a stranger you feel kinship with.
- Rituals need shared practice, not authentic origins. Sarah Josepha Hale campaigned for a national Thanksgiving explicitly to bind North and South and convinced Lincoln to establish it in **1863**, mid-Civil War. **46 million turkeys** are now eaten in the US on that one day, and there is **no historical record of turkey at the first Thanksgiving** — only roasted meat, deer and shellfish. The origin story was retro-fitted; the belonging is real anyway.
- Tension, stated plainly: rituals can be stressful and still work. A panicked late-night session to keep a streak alive is named as a genuine source of connection. The anxiety is not a side effect of the ritual — it is part of what produces the bond.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-to-support-a-language-learner (Duolingo blog, 2026-01-06; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The counterweight: Duolingo's guide for people supporting a learner leads with letting them choose, scripting the offer as a question rather than an imposition, and argues that control prevents burnout. It concedes that **"even highly motivated learners don't want to practice their new language all the time"** — the ceiling on voluntary effort is emotional, not a scheduling problem.

## The transferable pattern

- **Stop optimising impressions for an optional behaviour.** Reach answers "could they have acted." Attachment answers "did they want to." Only the second is your bottleneck.
- **Ship recurring people, not just features.** A recognisable recurring cast gives the user something to return *to* rather than something to get through, and it keeps working after the original objective is met.
- **Manufacture one shared marker.** A distinctive sound, a fixed daily act, a shared date. It lets strangers recognise each other, which turns an anonymous user base into a group people feel they belong to — and belonging is what makes them stay.
- **Do not wait for an authentic origin.** A ritual is legitimised by shared practice, not by history. You can start one deliberately this quarter and it will be real to whoever participates.
- **Accept that you cannot control the input side.** You will never win the share of attention you want against everything else. Design for partial, inconsistent participation still compounding, rather than for a schedule nobody keeps.
- **Hand the user the switch on effort.** Ask before defaulting to the demanding mode. Voluntary effort at a moment the user chose produces more total effort than obligation imposed at every moment — and attachment without autonomy just makes the pressure heavier.

Two costs. The ritual carries real stress, and a product that manufactures obligation is reaching for a different mechanism with its own failure modes — that is [[../duo-streak-mechanics/SKILL]], not this. And attachment work is slow, unattributable and hard to A/B test, which is exactly why teams reach for impressions instead.

## Apply to your product

- If you doubled your impressions tomorrow, would usage rise — or is every user already fully aware and simply not choosing to act?
- What recurring character, voice or presence does your product have that a user would notice the absence of?
- What is your shared marker — the sound, gesture or date that would make two of your users recognise each other in public — and could you simply decide on one this quarter?

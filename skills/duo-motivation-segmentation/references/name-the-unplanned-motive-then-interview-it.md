# Name the Unplanned Motive, Then Interview It

## Concept

Your users will adopt the product for reasons you never designed for. That is not a failure; it is the normal shape of growth. The failure is what happens next: a motive you have not named gets the default experience, and the default is tuned for the motives you did name. So your fastest-growing cohort silently receives your worst-fit product, and the chart that shows their growth gives you no way to know it. Naming the segment is cheap. Inferring its motive from the chart instead of asking is the expensive mistake.

## What Duolingo does

Duolingo's known motive set was school, work, travel, and culture. After February 2022 a fifth appeared that was in none of those buckets — solidarity.

- **More than 1.3 million people worldwide started learning Ukrainian**, with concentrated growth in Poland, Czechia, and Germany — the largest refugee-receiving countries — but with genuinely global reach, against a base of roughly **32M first-language and 44M total speakers**.
- Rather than infer the motive from the geography, Duolingo went and interviewed learners, including in New Zealand and the U.S., where no refugee-proximity explanation applied.
  Source: blog.duolingo.com/ukrainian-learner-stories (Duolingo blog, 2022-11-11; accessed 2026-09-22)
- Duolingo is explicit that scale destroys the free version of this signal. A Learning Designer who came from middle-school teaching: in a classroom "I could almost always tell which students didn't 'get it' by scanning the room," but with millions of users "we've got to use A/B tests, metrics, and targeted user experience research to figure out where we're teaching well and where we need to adjust" — at **thousands of daily active users per employee**, the face-reading channel simply does not exist. **Tension:** he names the human cost honestly — "you don't get the same swell of pride when a student reaches their goals." The people building the product lose the feedback that motivated them to build it, and the replacement instruments are slower and colder.
  Source: blog.duolingo.com/life-at-duolingo-parker-henry (Duolingo blog, 2023-09-29; accessed 2026-09-22)
- Where to look for the unnamed motive: the malformed inputs. Duolingo's reconstruction post makes the principle explicit — misspellings in journals, letters, and graffiti reveal how words actually sounded to the writer, irrespective of the standard spelling, and modern there/they're/their confusion will be equally revealing later. Correct input is overdetermined; it is consistent with understanding, copying, or guessing. A deviation is evidence about what someone actually believed.
  Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-languages-used-to-be-pronounced (Duolingo blog, 2024-10-29; accessed 2026-09-22)

## The transferable pattern

Build a standing loop for motives you did not plan for.

1. **Watch for growth you cannot explain with your existing dimensions.** A cohort that is growing fast and whose declared reason is the catch-all is the strongest candidate.
2. **Name it before you understand it.** A provisional label gets the segment into dashboards, experiment splits, and planning conversations; an unnamed segment is invisible by construction.
3. **Interview outside the obvious explanation.** Talk to the part of the cohort your geographic or demographic story does *not* cover — that is where the real motive is exposed.
4. **Mine the failures, not the successes.** Failed searches, abandoned flows, typo'd queries, and visible workarounds tell you what people expected. Correct usage only proves they complied with your interface.
5. **Budget the replacement instruments deliberately.** Past the scale where anyone can watch individual users, qualitative signal stops arriving for free. Nothing forces you to notice it is missing.

## Apply to your product

- Which cohort grew fastest last quarter, and can you state its reason for being here in one sentence without guessing?
- When did someone on your team last speak to a user who was not in your target segment?
- Do you log the malformed inputs — failed searches, abandoned steps, workarounds — or only the successful path?

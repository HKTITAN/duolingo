# Split by Cohort Before Concluding What Users Want

## Concept

An aggregate ranking is dominated by the largest cohort and by whatever has universal appeal, which makes it look like consensus. It usually isn't. Splitting the same ranking by cohort tells you whether you are looking at one preference or several opposing ones averaged together — and the items that move position between cohorts are precisely the ones where a differentiated product decision is available. The item at the top, identical everywhere, is the one that tells you nothing about who to build for next.

## What Duolingo does

Duolingo's cross-generational analysis kept the top row fixed and let the rest of the ranking move.

- **Spanish is #1 for every U.S. age group** — the consensus item. Underneath it, the ranking inverts: **Japanese is #3 for ages 13–22 and falls to #6 for 40+; Korean peaks at #6 for 13–17 and drops out of the top 10 for 60+; Italian moves the other way — #7 for 13–17, #6 for 18–39, #4 for 40+.**
- The age distribution explains why the aggregate is dominated the way it is: **~60% of U.S. learners are under 30**, with a sharp drop between 30-somethings and 40-somethings — 13–17 ≈ 22%, 18–22 ≈ 20%, 23–29 ≈ 18%, 30–39 ≈ 16%, 40–49 ≈ 10%, 50–59 ≈ 8%, 60+ ≈ 7%. Motivation splits the same way: Italian learners cite travel at 22% and brain training at 17%.
- The piece exists because a reader asked. Duolingo's "Dear Duolingo" column answers reader-submitted questions with first-party data — material no competitor and no journalist can produce, with the framing supplied free by the person who wrote in.
  Source: blog.duolingo.com/dear-duolingo-how-does-language-learning-differ-between-generations (Duolingo blog, 2022-05-03; accessed 2026-09-22)
- The inbox itself is a demand signal, weighted by intensity. The column received **well over 1,000 questions in under a year while answering 27** — a backlog of 36+ years at that rate. German learners submitted by far the most, and Duolingo responded by pulling in a German-specialist PhD. Topic distribution: 5 posts on pronunciation, 4 on Spanish, 3 on grammatical gender, 2 data deep-dives. The generational data piece was the top post of 2022.
  Source: blog.duolingo.com/one-year-dear-duolingo (Duolingo blog, 2022-12-27; accessed 2026-09-22)

**Tension.** Splitting has a floor. The smallest U.S. band here is roughly 7% of learners, and each further split divides the sample again — so a cohort cut fine enough to be interesting is usually too thin to be stable. Say which cuts you trust and which are directional.

## The transferable pattern

Never conclude "users want X" from a pooled ranking.

1. **Publish the split, not the aggregate.** If you show one table, show the one with cohorts as columns. The aggregate will be read as a mandate.
2. **Read the inversions, not the top row.** The universally popular item is table stakes. The items that swap places between cohorts are where a targeted decision earns something.
3. **Check the mix before believing the average.** When one cohort is 60% of your base, the aggregate is that cohort's opinion wearing everyone's name.
4. **Treat an open question inbox as unmet demand, segmented for free.** Which cohort writes in most, and about what, is information usage analytics cannot produce — analytics only shows what people did with what you already built.
5. **Name your sample floor.** Below it, report direction and stop reporting rank.

## Apply to your product

- Take your top feature-request list. Split it by your two largest cohorts — does the order below #1 survive?
- Which cohort is large enough to be silently setting your aggregate numbers?
- Where do unsolicited questions from users land today, and does anyone count them by segment?

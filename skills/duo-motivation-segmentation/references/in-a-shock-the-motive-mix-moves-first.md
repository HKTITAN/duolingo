# In a Shock, the Motive Mix Moves First

## Concept

When something external disrupts your users' lives, the tempting read of a traffic surge is "more of the same, faster." It almost never is. New users arriving under changed circumstances are a different population with different jobs to be done, and the two signals that reveal this — the mix of declared reasons and the hour-of-day curve — move before the headline volume number tells you anything interesting. If you onboard a surge into a product shaped for your old modal user, you will lose it when the shock recedes, and the churn will look like a retention problem rather than what it was: a segmentation error made at the top of the funnel.

## What Duolingo does

Duolingo tracked new learners' self-reported motivation and time-of-day behaviour through March 2020 and reported the mix shift alongside the volume shift.

- **The motive mix moved sharply.** School rose to over **30% of new learners, up 7 points**; brain training became #2 at over **15%**; travel collapsed from #1 to #3 at under **13%**. In some markets the shift was far larger: school users exceeded **50% of new users in Mexico** and **40% in Chile and France**.
- **The daily rhythm moved with it.** Commute-time study was replaced by midday study — **midday lessons rose 1.5–2x, up to 2.5x in the U.K.** New learners spent **13% more time** than historically typical, and **stopped distinguishing weekdays from weekends**.
- **Composition changed inside the volume.** In the U.S., new users choosing to study English fell from **19% in February to 11% at the March peak** — the surge was not more of the previous cohort.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/changes-in-duolingo-usage-during-the-covid-19-pandemic (Duolingo blog, 2020-04-08; accessed 2026-09-22)

**Tension.** Every figure here is a snapshot taken inside a moving event, and the motive field is self-reported — people reach for the socially legible reason under stress, and "school" was the legible reason that spring. Reading a shock in real time means acting on a mix that may itself be a temporary artifact of how people were describing their situation, not just of what they were doing. The honest posture is to instrument and watch, and to avoid rebuilding the product around a mix that has not yet held for a full cycle.

## The transferable pattern

Treat a surge as a composition question before you treat it as a capacity question.

1. **Re-read the declared-motive mix first.** Share by reason, week over week. A shift of several points in the modal reason is a bigger fact than the volume line above it.
2. **Re-read the hour-of-day and day-of-week curves.** When people's routines break, the time they reach for you changes, and any scheduled surface — reminders, digests, batch jobs, staffing — is now aimed at a rhythm that no longer exists.
3. **Check composition inside the total.** A flat mix with a rising total is genuinely more of the same. A shifting mix with a rising total is a new population wearing your old metrics.
4. **Do not rebuild for the surge yet.** Onboard it well, instrument it hard, and wait to see whether the mix persists past the disruption before committing the roadmap to it.
5. **Write down the pre-shock baseline while you still have it.** The comparison is the entire finding, and it gets harder to reconstruct every week.

## Apply to your product

- If your signups doubled next month, which report would tell you within a week whether it was a new population or an old one at higher volume?
- Do you have a stored baseline of your declared-reason mix and your hour-of-day curve from a normal period?
- Which of your scheduled surfaces assumes a routine — commute, workday, weekend — that a disruption would break?

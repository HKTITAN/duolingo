# Analogy, Plus Its Exception Clause

## Concept

An analogy lets someone reuse a schema they already have, so a new rule costs recognition instead of memorization. Two things make the difference between an analogy that works and one that misleads. First, it has to map the **whole contrast** — both sides of the distinction at once — rather than illustrate one side vividly and leave the other undefined. An abstract boundary has to be held in working memory as a rule, but a scene can be simulated, and the user reads the answer off the simulation. Second, it has to declare its own limits. An analogy delivered as law hardens into a superstition the user cannot override when the situation actually calls for the exception.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/spanish-preterite-imperfect (Duolingo blog, 2021-08-10; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo teaches the preterite/imperfect contrast as a stage. The curtain opens on a town — people walking, a fountain trickling — all imperfect, the standing scenery you expect to continue. Then a girl walks on, twirls, sings — all preterite, the drama in the foreground "under that magnifying glass." Both sides of the contrast are in one scene, so the learner never has to decide which half of the metaphor they are in.
- Why it works: foreground versus background is a distinction people already operate flawlessly, so the new one inherits that fluency rather than competing for working memory.
- The same post renders intensity visually rather than verbally, using a one-to-three repeated-face scale to show that gradable properties stack and non-gradable ones do not — the structure of the rule is carried by the picture.
- Duolingo's chess guidance runs the same play in a different domain: piece development is explained as soccer players staying huddled at their own goalposts, and centre control by asking the reader to see the flat board as a mountain whose peak is the four central squares. It then closes by stating that every guideline has exceptions (blog.duolingo.com/chess-opening-moves-tips (Duolingo blog, 2025-10-16; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- That closing line is the load-bearing part, not a disclaimer. Naming the guidelines as breakable is what keeps a beginner's heuristic from becoming a ceiling — you need the rule before you can break it, and you need permission to break it before you can stop being a beginner.
- Tension: every analogy imports baggage from the source domain along with the structure. The stage metaphor carries an implication of narrative intent that the underlying distinction does not have. An analogy is a scaffold with a scheduled demolition date, not a definition.

## The transferable pattern

- **Map the contrast, not the concept.** If your metaphor illustrates only option A, users will pick A whenever they are unsure. One scene should place both options where their difference is obvious.
- **Prefer a scene that can be simulated to a rule that must be recalled.** Spatial and physical relations — foreground/background, container/contents, upstream/downstream, peak/slope — are run, not remembered.
- **Borrow from a domain your users actually operate in**, not from the one that flatters your product. A metaphor from an unfamiliar domain is just a second thing to learn.
- **Ship the exception clause with the rule.** "This is a guideline and here is where it stops holding" costs one sentence and prevents the far more expensive failure: a confident user applying a beginner's heuristic in the case it was never meant for.
- **Let the picture carry the structure** when the rule is about magnitude, overlap or containment. Redrawing the relation beats describing it.

## Apply to your product

- Take the analogy in your onboarding. Does it show both options of the choice the user has to make, or only the one you wish they picked?
- Which of your beginner heuristics has hardened into a rule your advanced users are afraid to break — and where did you fail to say it was breakable?
- What does your analogy imply about your product that is not true, and when in the user's journey should you retire it?

# Anchor to a Reflex They Already Run

## Concept

A description in your domain's vocabulary gives the user a fact to store. A mapping onto a reaction they already perform involuntarily gives them something they can already execute — retrieval then runs on equipment they own instead of on a rule they memorized last Tuesday. The same logic extends to partial and degraded versions: if the user already performs a stripped-down form of the behaviour somewhere, start there. The new concept becomes an extension of an existing competence rather than a new category to build, and the emotional reading shifts from "this is alien" to "I already do a version of this."

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/chinese-tones (Duolingo blog, 2026-04-28; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Rather than teaching Mandarin's tone contours as pitch curves, Duolingo anchors each of the **4 tones to one familiar English utterance** the reader already produces without thinking: Tone 1 is the doctor's "Ahhhh…", Tone 2 an incredulous "What?!?", Tone 3 a coy "Well…", Tone 4 a forceful "No!". Nothing about pitch notation is taught; the reader runs a reflex they have and reads the contour off it.
- The noun-cases post uses the vestigial version. Before introducing German's four cases, it shows that English pronouns are the leftovers of a real case system — *she/her*, *he/him* are surviving nominative and accusative forms. The reader is not learning a new category; they are being shown one they already operate in a reduced form.
- The scale it is placed against is explicit: **Russian and Latin have six cases, German four, Finnish fifteen, Hungarian more than a dozen, Chinese none.** The vestigial anchor is what makes fifteen readable instead of terrifying (blog.duolingo.com/what-are-noun-cases (Duolingo blog, 2022-08-04; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The same post models an overlapping-but-not-identical mapping visually rather than verbally: Spanish *en* is drawn as one circle overlapping English's three (*in* / *on* / *at*), so the shape of the mismatch is the thing shown.
- Tension: an anchor is an approximation. "Tone 2 is an incredulous What?!?" is close enough to get a beginner producing a rising contour and not close enough to be correct in every context. An anchor that is never retired hardens into a wrong model, so it has to be built to be outgrown — which is the same discipline as naming an analogy's exceptions.

## The transferable pattern

- **Look for the reflex before you write the definition.** Ask what your user already does automatically — in another tool, in physical life, in a habit — that has the same shape as the thing you are introducing.
- **A degraded version still counts.** A partial, vestigial or accidental instance of the behaviour is a better starting point than a clean definition, because recognition is cheaper than construction.
- **Anchor with an action, not a description.** "It behaves like the thing you do when X" beats "it is a persistent, hierarchical container," because the first can be executed and the second must be stored.
- **Show the shape of the mismatch.** When your concept only partially overlaps theirs, depict the overlap rather than describing it — a picture of two circles carries the boundary that a paragraph loses.
- **Plan the anchor's retirement.** Every anchor is approximate. Decide where it breaks and where in the user's journey you replace it, or you will spend later releases fighting the model you installed.

## Apply to your product

- Take the concept your users most often get wrong. What do they already do automatically, somewhere else, that has the same structure?
- Does your product already contain a stripped-down version of a concept you introduce later as if it were new — and could you point at it instead?
- For each analogy already in your onboarding, where does it break, and what happens to the user who keeps applying it past that point?

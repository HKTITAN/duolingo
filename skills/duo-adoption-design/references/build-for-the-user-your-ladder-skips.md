# Build for the User Your Ladder Skips

## Concept

Onboarding and progression assume a single scalar level. Real users are lumpy: fluent on one axis, absent on another. That user is bored and patronised by the beginner track and lost on the expert track, so both tracks lose them — and they are often a large, motivated, high-intent segment rather than an edge case. The same failure repeats at the top of the ladder in a different form: advanced users are still being trained on clean, canonical examples when the thing that separates them from competence is handling the mess.

## What Duolingo does

- Duolingo's treatment of **heritage speakers** — people who understand a language fluently but cannot readily produce it — names the problem directly: standard classes are designed for true beginners, and this group fits neither end.
- The segment is not small. **About 70% of Latinos in the U.S. use Spanish at home** (Pew).
- The structural response already exists outside Duolingo and is cited as precedent: **U.S. universities have started creating dedicated heritage-speaker tracks with different pacing** — a separate ladder, not a different starting rung on the same one.
- At the advanced end, Duolingo built Stories in the formats people actually encounter rather than idealized dialogue: **newspaper articles, newsletters, customer reviews, letters, emails, websites, ads and brochures**.
- The targeted skills are the ones clean material strips out: detecting a stance, telling whether someone is joking, weighing options to reach a decision.
- Scope at launch: **4 courses** — Spanish and French for English speakers, English for Spanish and Portuguese speakers.

## The transferable pattern

Your progression model probably has one dimension. Your users have several. Find the asymmetric segment by asking where people with obviously relevant experience still fail your onboarding, or where confident users quit early.

Then decide honestly between two options:

1. **Average it.** Put the asymmetric user somewhere in the middle. This is cheap, and it is why they leave — half the content insults them and half is over their head.
2. **Build the separate track.** Different pacing, a different entry point, and content that assumes the strength while addressing the gap. More expensive, and the only thing that actually works for a segment this size.

At the far end of any ladder the correction is different: stop teaching with clean examples. Advanced competence is mostly about handling the variation that a clean example removes — tone, implied stance, irony, format conventions, ambiguity, missing context. Continuing to train on idealized material produces users who are expert in your product and helpless outside it. Swap in the real artifacts: the messy inputs, the ambiguous cases, the badly-formatted real-world examples your users will actually meet.

One warning that pairs with this: do not promote a specialist segment's needs into the default flow. A separate track is a separate track.

## Apply to your product

- Which of your users are strong on one dimension and absent on another? Are they currently averaged into a level that fits neither half?
- Would a separate entry point serve them better than a different starting position on the same ladder — and how large is that segment really?
- At the advanced end of your product, are you still showing clean canonical examples? What are the messy real inputs your users face that you have never put in front of them?

# One Ordered Path Beats a Branching Surface

## Concept

A branching home screen looks generous — pick anything, go at your own pace. What it actually does is hand the user a strategy problem they have no basis to solve. Every session then carries a low-grade doubt that the effort is landing in the wrong place, and doubt suppresses return visits far more quietly than difficulty does. When users start asking whether they are doing it "the right way," the fix is almost never better documentation. It is one ordered sequence where the next step is always obvious. The sequencing is the product; the raw material was usually already free.

## What Duolingo does

- Replaced the branching skill "tree" home screen with a single linear path of circles, rolled out to all learners on **November 1, 2022**. The stated trigger was direct user feedback: learners often said they were not sure whether they were using Duolingo the "correct" or "best" way.
- The conversion was mechanical and published: **one path circle = one crown level** of a skill on the old home screen. That is why the path looks visibly longer than the tree it replaced.
- Demand-side confirmation from learner interviews: a learner who chose Japanese specifically because its writing system was unfamiliar described the appeal as not having to devise a program — someone had already done the homework. Learners profiled in that set report streaks up to **1,828 days** (5+ years) and top-1% usage.
- A travel learner, a brewery co-owner, said she needed something where she does not have to think about what to do or which bits to learn. After **five weeks** she could read menus and signs and order food.
- Tension, named by Duolingo itself: the path is visibly longer, conversation mode in Stories was removed "for now," and per-circle labels disappeared — tap-to-reveal popups and rewritten unit headers had to be added back to compensate.
- Tension, named by the users: the same travel learner lost the visual overview of full verb tables that a classroom gives, so knowledge accumulates without her being able to see its structure. And the self-directed power user loses their own route entirely.

## The transferable pattern

The activation energy of an open-ended goal is dominated by planning, and planning is paid up front before any progress is felt — a pure loss. A pre-sequenced default moves the first rewarding moment to within seconds of opening the product, which is what lets a habit form at all.

So: when you catch users asking whether they are using the product correctly, treat it as a structural signal, not a docs gap. Collapse the branching surface into one default order, and make the single next action unmissable on open.

Two costs come with it, and you should price them before you ship:

1. **The work becomes visible.** A linear sequence exposes its own length. Users who never saw the size of the job now see it.
2. **You destroy the overview.** A branching map was also a map. Removing it removes the user's mental model of the whole. Put the structure back somewhere — labels on demand, a collapsed outline, a summary view — or people accumulate progress they cannot see the shape of.

The self-directed expert loses most here. They need a relief valve, which is a separate design problem.

## Apply to your product

- Where in your product does the user have to decide what to do next with no basis for deciding? What would the single correct default be?
- If you collapsed that surface into one order tomorrow, which users would lose a route they had built for themselves — and what do you give them instead?
- Your onboarding or home screen: is the first rewarding moment seconds away, or is it behind a planning step the user pays before feeling anything?

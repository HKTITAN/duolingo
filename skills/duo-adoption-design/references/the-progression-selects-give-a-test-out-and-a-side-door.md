# The Progression Selects; Give a Test-Out and a Side Door

## Concept

Once you order the track, two demands arrive immediately: "let me pick" and "let me skip." Granting the first breaks the thing that made the ordering valuable — the match between what the content demands and what the user can currently do. Granting the second is fine, as long as the user pays for the skip with a demonstration rather than a click. And a third, rarer user shows up with a self-diagnosis: they know exactly what they are bad at. A progress track answers "what is next." It structurally cannot answer that.

## What Duolingo does

- In the Music path, Duolingo pins **one selected song per section** and does not let learners browse and choose, "because we want to make sure the song is relevant to the notes and skills you're learning."
- The escape hatch is a demonstration, not a menu: to reach new songs you must **complete a quick test of your skills to skip ahead to a new unit**.
- Tension, and a sharp one: this directly denies the single most-requested behaviour for a music product. "Can I choose the song I want to play?" — no. Duolingo traded an obvious, loudly-stated user desire for difficulty fit.
- The side door is a separate surface. The Practice Hub lets learners pick the skill and exercise type on demand — review past Mistakes, study recommended Words, Speak, Listen, Focused Listening, Stories, Radio — entirely outside the main path.
- That surface was **made free to all learners**, following Explain My Answer moving from paid to free. The on-demand repair route was judged too important to adoption to sit behind a paywall.

## The transferable pattern

Free selection destroys the invariant. If an item is only useful at a particular point in the user's development, letting them grab it out of order converts a useful item into a merely pleasant one, and the value you engineered evaporates without anyone noticing.

Three moves, in order:

1. **Let the progression select.** The system picks the item; the user's choice is whether to proceed.
2. **Make the skip a test, not a button.** A test-out preserves agency and the invariant at once: the user may jump ahead exactly as far as they can demonstrate. This is the answer to "this is too slow for me" that does not cost you the ordering.
3. **Build a separate on-demand surface for self-diagnosed gaps.** A user who arrives knowing their own weakness is in a rare and valuable state — they brought both the intent and the diagnosis. Forcing them back onto the default order wastes the intent and teaches them the product cannot respond to what they actually want.

Expect the denial in step 1 to be your most-requested feature forever. That is not evidence you are wrong; it is evidence the constraint is load-bearing. Say out loud why you deny it, in the product, at the moment the user wants it.

## Apply to your product

- Which items in your product are only valuable at a specific point in the user's development? What breaks if a new user reaches one of them first?
- What would a test-out look like for you — what demonstration would honestly justify letting someone skip ahead?
- Where does a user who already knows their own weak spot go? If the answer is "back to the default flow," you are wasting your most motivated sessions.

# Rank What You Teach by Cost of Getting It Wrong

## Concept

Completeness is the default organizing principle and it is almost always wrong. A syllabus sorted for coverage front-loads whatever comes first in the reference material, spends the user's scarcest hours on items that never determine an outcome, and — worse — signals that the whole inventory is required. Users abandon when the syllabus feels infinite. Sort by consequence instead: which errors cost the user the task, and which merely deviate from the ideal. The first hours then buy the largest share of real capability, and the user stays long enough to reach the finicky material later. The corollary is that you have to tell them which deviations are safe to keep.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/grammar-practice-tips (Duolingo blog, 2022-05-17; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo's curriculum designers say plainly that **not all rules are equally important for communicating**: rules for asking questions or expressing past and future matter a lot, whereas **using the wrong noun gender usually still leaves you understood**. The same ranking runs inside exceptions — learn common irregulars like eat/ate before rarer ones like break/broke.
- The pronunciation guidance ranks defects by whether they break the outcome. **Mixing b and p turns "beso" (kiss) into "peso" (weight) and must be fixed. Pronouncing the d in "cada" the English way "might sound a little off to native speakers, but it won't interfere with communication"** — so it is explicitly left alone (blog.duolingo.com/improve-pronunciation-in-new-language-tips (Duolingo blog, 2025-04-09; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- **The priority order is not stable.** The same post notes that for intermediate and advanced learners, changing the rhythm and melody of speech improves comprehensibility more than fine-tuning individual sounds. What counts as a meaning-breaking defect shifts as the user improves, so a single fixed ranking will be wrong for somebody.
- Consequence-ranking also scopes recognition versus production. Trip-prep guidance tells learners to work out which items they only need to **recognize** on menus and signs — "often recognizing words will be enough!" — to **skip large numbers entirely** because prices are written in digits they can already read, and to **drill 1–10 hard** because those are what they will actually say aloud to restaurant staff (blog.duolingo.com/learn-language-for-trip (Duolingo blog, 2023-04-19; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- **The honest cost, stated in the post:** "there's bound to be words, local slang, and regional dialects you won't understand when you get to your destination. And that's ok!" Consequence-ranking deliberately ships a user with known gaps. Duolingo also concedes some items simply have to be memorized as facts with no shortcut, and that unstructured exposure over time is what eventually fills in what the curriculum skipped.

## The transferable pattern

Build the inventory, then score every item on two axes: **how often does this come up**, and **what does getting it wrong cost**. Teach the high-cost items first regardless of where they sit in the reference structure. Let the low-cost errors persist, and say so.

Three consequences of taking this seriously:

- **Some correctness is not worth buying.** If an error does not change the outcome, correcting it spends the user's attention for no return and crowds out an error that does. Publish the "safe to get wrong" list — it is as valuable as the syllabus.
- **Recognition is often sufficient.** Training someone to produce what they only ever need to identify is the most common way to waste a preparation budget. Decide per item which one the job requires.
- **Re-rank by tier.** The factor that decides a beginner's outcome is not the factor that decides an expert's. A ranking that never changes is a ranking that is wrong for one of them.

Users do not need a complete model to be effective. They need the part of the model that carries the consequences, plus permission to be incomplete about the rest.

## Apply to your product

- List the ten things your product teaches. For each, what actually breaks if the user gets it wrong — the outcome, or only the aesthetics?
- Which items do your users need to *recognize* rather than produce? Are you training production for any of them?
- What would your "safe to get wrong" list say, and are you willing to publish it?

# Novelty Entry Products

## Concept

A novelty offering attracts people the main funnel never reaches. It is cheap to build because it is small, and it has no practical utility, so the entire business case rests on one measurement: how many of the people it pulls in go on to use the real thing. That crossover rate is what separates a growth channel from a vanity project, and a team that cannot state it is running the vanity version. The second discipline is timing: an offering that exists because of a fandom ships on the fandom's calendar, not on yours, because that is when the audience is already assembled and already talking.

## What Duolingo does

- **Measures the crossover, and publishes it.** Duolingo runs courses in Klingon, High Valyrian and Esperanto, and reports that **43% of users who join to study a constructed language go on to study another language** on the platform. In 2020, Duolingo Events hosted **over 1,000 Esperanto language exchanges** — the novelty cohort turned out to be an unusually social one. Source: blog.duolingo.com/why-learn-a-made-up-language (Duolingo blog, 2021-05-11; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **Ships on the fandom's clock.** High Valyrian launched in 2019, built with conlanger David J. Peterson, and by July 2022 had **514,000 active learners**. Duolingo timed the course expansion — **159 new vocabulary words and 5 new skills**, including commanding dragons — to the *House of the Dragon* premiere, paired with a free-month promo code. The post concedes the utility case outright: you are not going to travel to Westeros, and you likely will not need it for a job. Source: blog.duolingo.com/high-valyrian-course-updates (Duolingo blog, 2022-07-19; accessed 2026-09-22)

Tension: the crossover rate is the only argument, so it has to be instrumented before the thing ships, not reconstructed afterward. And a release calendar set by someone else's franchise is a real operational cost — you are committing engineering capacity to a date you do not control and cannot move.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Build the small thing that has no defensible reason to exist, and defend it with one number.** Attention-grabbing launches reach people your positioning never will. State the conversion rate into the core product, or do not build it.
2. **Instrument the crossover on day one.** Retrofitted attribution on a novelty launch produces an unfalsifiable story about brand halo.
3. **Borrow an existing audience's calendar.** Shipping into someone else's attention spike means the distribution is already paid for by them.
4. **Do not apologize for the lack of utility.** Naming it plainly is what makes the audience trust the rest.

## Apply to your product

- Is there a small, strange, low-cost version of your product that would reach a group your main funnel never touches?
- If you shipped it, what single number would you check in six months to decide whether it was a channel or a toy?
- Whose calendar — a release, a season, an event — could you ship into instead of picking your own date?

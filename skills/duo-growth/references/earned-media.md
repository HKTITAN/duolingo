# Earned Media

## Concept

Earned media is press coverage you didn't pay for. Most companies treat it as luck. The discipline is to *engineer* the conditions under which it happens — make moves that journalists already cover, give them the artifacts (quote-able lines, screenshot-able moments, character cameos) they need to write the story, and let the brand carry across into the article.

## What Duolingo does

- [[marketing-stunts]] are designed for press: each one has a one-line summary a journalist can lift, an image they can use, and a hook tied to the cultural moment.
- The brand voice ([[../duo-voice/references/wholesome-unhinged]]) is itself news-worthy — articles about "the Duolingo owl threatening users" write themselves.
- Quarterly results, product launches, and brand moments are paired with social content that primes the press cycle (the TikTok video drops the day before the press release).
- The PR team plays both sides: pitching the journalists who'd write the piece, and hosting the screenshots/clips/quotes the journalists will need.

## The transferable pattern

Three rules:

1. **Give journalists artifacts, not press releases.** A quote-able line, a strong image, a clear angle. Press releases get filed; artifacts get shared.
2. **The brand is the angle.** Without a brand, your news is generic. With a brand, every product update is an opportunity to extend the story.
3. **Time the loop.** Social content primes the audience. Press coverage rides the priming. In-app moments reward the resulting traffic. Sequence matters.

Anti-pattern: pitching every product update as news. Press fatigue is real; the second pitch is harder than the first.

## Apply to your product

- The last time you launched something, did you give the press an artifact, a press release, or just a blog post?
- Is your brand a news angle, or do you require the news to come from the product?
- When was your last earned-media cycle? Was it engineered or accidental?

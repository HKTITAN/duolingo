# Persona as Generator

## Concept

A trait list is a description. It answers "what is this character like?" and nothing else, so every situation nobody scripted becomes a guess — and each guess that rings false costs credibility. A persona built as a *desire blocked by an obstacle* is a generator: it produces situations, jokes and reactions on its own, because friction is where stories come from. The surplus biography you never publish is what lets any writer, designer or model derive correct behaviour in a case you never designed for.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/linda-simensky-duocon-interview (Duolingo blog, 2022-08-30; accessed 2026-09-22)

Duolingo Studios builds out each character's passions, motivations and goals, "as well as determining what gets in the way for each character." Writers are then required to keep everyone "literally 'in character' in any situation" — the deliverable is not a bio page, it is the ability to answer novel cases consistently.

- **The obstacle is the engine.** Lin is easy-going and avoids obligations, wanting the freedom to get up and go — which keeps her out of a steady job or a schedule; her grandmother Lucy wants her to figure it out and move out. In Simensky's words, "that's often where the stories and the humor can start."
- **The falseness test is concrete.** Head of animation Linda Simensky's check on whether a depiction is in character: Eddy in a tie, enjoying office work, "wouldn't ring true." No rulebook produces that verdict; a model of the character does.
- **Density shows fast.** Simensky's read on the cast: "Five short seconds of animation told me everything about each character." Five seconds is the budget a real audience gives you.
- **Instructional casts need an in-world advocate.** Simensky's rule for educational content: make sure "there was a main character in every series who was particularly enthusiastic about whatever the curriculum was for that series," so the subject has an emotional carrier instead of reading as an imposition.

**Tension.** The development cost does not drop for background characters — "you need the same amount of development and information for all characters, no matter what sort of content you're making." Most of that work never ships, and the enthusiast role can slide into being a mouthpiece if the character has no desire of their own.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Write the want and the thing blocking it** before you write a single adjective. Adjectives are downstream.
2. **Over-invest in unpublished backstory.** The ratio of written to shown is the margin you have when something unplanned happens.
3. **Ship a falseness test, not a style guide.** One concrete "this would never happen" example teaches more than a page of traits.
4. **If your cast carries instruction, cast one genuine enthusiast** for the subject — otherwise the audience adopts the cast's boredom rather than your intent.

## Apply to your product

- For each persona or mascot you use, what do they want, and what is stopping them?
- Could a new teammate answer "what would this character do here?" for a situation your guidelines never mention?
- If your characters exist to explain something, does any of them actually like the subject?

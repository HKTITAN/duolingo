# Recurring Cast in Content

## Concept

High-volume informational content is a commodity: any competitor can publish the same facts. What cannot be cheaply copied is a reader's accumulated familiarity with your characters. A rigid page template makes production cheap; a fixed cast appearing in every example makes the page recognizably yours rather than an interchangeable search result. And inside a single piece, one concrete, personally-staked scenario running end to end gives every new rule something to attach to instead of floating free.

## What Duolingo does

**The template.** Every guide in this shard runs the same skeleton: an "In this post" table of contents, callout boxes for each rule, comparison tables, illustrated diagrams, internal links to sibling guides, and a pun sign-off — "Your English is getting progressively stronger!", "Possessive adjectives are your time to shine!"

**The cast.** Example sentences across unrelated posts use the same **10+** recurring named characters — Lily, Zari, Junior, Eddy, Oscar, Lin, Vikram, Bea, Lucy, Duo — with personalities that hold across authors. Junior wants ice cream for every meal in both the pronouns post and an unrelated Olympics post. The consistency survives **at least 8 different bylined authors**, because the cast is the shared asset, not any one writer's invention.

**The running example.** The Spanish commands guide is built entirely around the author's own goal of running the Chicago Marathon, and every single example is a reminder she actually set on her phone: *Ve al gimnasio*, *Corre por 30 minutos*, *No corras todos los días*, *No uses ropa de algodón para correr*. The affirmative-versus-negative distinction stops being a rule and becomes her do's list and her don'ts list.

**Tension.** A rigid template plus a fixed cast caps how far any single piece can deviate, and the pun sign-off is a tax on writers who have nothing to pun about. The familiarity payoff also only arrives at volume — with a handful of posts a year, nobody accumulates recognition and you have paid the production constraint for nothing.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Write the template once and enforce it.** The same sections in the same order is what makes volume affordable.
2. **Keep a cast file and make every writer use it.** Names, personalities, and running gags belong to the publication, not the author.
3. **Give each piece one staked scenario** with real consequences, and run every example through it. Stakes are what make examples stick.
4. **Ship a signature closing move** so the last thing a reader sees is unmistakably yours.

## Apply to your product

- Could a reader tell your documentation from a competitor's with the logo removed?
- Do your examples use the same names and situations across pieces, or fresh placeholders each time?
- Pick your most-visited explainer: is there one scenario running through it, or twenty disconnected illustrations?

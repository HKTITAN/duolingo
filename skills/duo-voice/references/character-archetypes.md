# Character Archetypes

## Concept

A character archetype is a constraint on voice. Each character has a register (earnest / chaotic / professorial / etc.), a set of topics they speak to, and an emotional range. Voice consistency at scale becomes a casting problem instead of a copy problem: not "what would the brand say?" but "who in the cast is saying this, and what are they allowed to say?"

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/building-character (Duolingo blog, 2020-11-10; accessed 2026-09-22) · blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-female-character-origin-stories (Duolingo blog, 2022-03-08; accessed 2026-09-22)

The cast took **18 months** to design, and the spread is deliberate:

- **Duo** — chaotic, the engine of the unhinged half of the brand.
- **Lily** — deadpan, introverted, "self-protective sarcasm."
- **Zari** — super outgoing and type-A; "probably in 10 clubs at school and president of 8 of them."
- **Eddy** — confident, athletic, golden-retriever energy.
- **Junior** — child, used for content that needs softness.

Two mechanisms hold it together:

- **Opposition pairs.** Characters are cast against each other to generate conflict for free — outgoing Zari with introverted Lily, laid-back Lin with neurotic Bea. "There's a lot of natural drama generated from opposites attracting."
- **Traits borrowed from real people.** The creative team pulled character traits from friends, sisters and mothers in their own lives rather than assembling characters out of demographic slots. Recognition attaches to a trait as specific as Lily's self-protective sarcasm, because a reader can name who it reminds them of; a character defined only by which boxes they fill has nothing for recognition to grip. The team held two goals in tension here — subverting stereotypes while keeping the characters real — and resolved it by prioritising realness and letting specificity do the anti-stereotype work.
- **One shared construction.** Every human is built from Duo's four components (geometric body-plus-wings silhouette, oversized eyes, distinct body shape, detached feet), which is what lets a culturally diverse human cast share a world with a green owl. Each character then gets a unique animation on a correct answer, plus mid-lesson interstitials.

Each character's pose set, color palette, and dialogue register are different enough that copywriters and designers can ask "who would say this?" and get a clear answer.

## The transferable pattern

Three rules for a useful cast:

1. **At least two contrasting registers.** A monolithic cast collapses into one voice; a contrasting cast lets you place the right line on the right speaker.
2. **Each character has a *what they don't say* list.** Constraints make the voice legible. Lily would never be earnest; Zari would never be sarcastic.
3. **Pose / illustration / animation system per character.** Voice without a face fades; a face without a voice gets generic. Build them together.

A useful test: hand a piece of copy to a teammate without context. Can they tell which character said it? If everyone could have said it, the cast isn't doing work.

## Apply to your product

- If your brand is a cast of one (one logo, one voice), would adding a second persona help?
- If you have characters or personas, what does each one *not* say?
- Could a teammate identify your character from a single line of dialogue?

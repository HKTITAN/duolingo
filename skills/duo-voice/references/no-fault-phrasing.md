# No-Fault Phrasing

## Concept

Two sentences can report the same event and land as different social acts. "You forgot your keys" assigns agency; "the keys got forgotten on you" reports the same fact while leaving the person out of the causal slot. The information is identical, the identity cost is not — and the identity cost is what raises the price of the next attempt. Copy that assigns agency for a failure triggers defensiveness; blameless construction keeps people trying.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/5-meanings-of-spanish-se (Duolingo blog, 2024-08-22; accessed 2026-09-22) · blog.duolingo.com/mistakes-in-french-for-learners (Duolingo blog, 2026-05-28; accessed 2026-09-22)

Duolingo's editorial team spotlights the accidental or no-fault *se* as perhaps the most wonderful of the construction's five documented uses — and the reason is exactly this social one.

- **"Se me olvidaron las llaves"** is literally *the keys were forgotten to me*, not *I forgot the keys*.
- **"Se me rompió el vaso"** is *the glass broke on me*, not *I broke the glass*.

The construction distances the doer from the action while still naming who it happened to — so the sentence stays informative and personal without being an accusation. It is a grammaticalized version of a choice every product writer makes in every failure string.

The companion move, covered in [[error-copy]], is normalizing the failure before explaining the fix: the common-mistakes posts open by calling errors "a natural—and even necessary!—part of the learning journey" and noting that others are working through the same ones, and only then list them.

**Tension.** Blameless phrasing is evasive when the user genuinely needs to change something — if the next step depends on an action they took, hiding the cause hides the fix. It is also the standard construction for a company dodging its own responsibility ("your payment could not be processed"), so the rule cuts both ways: remove blame from the user, keep it on yourself.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Check the subject of every failure sentence.** If the person is named as the cause of a bad outcome, rewrite so the outcome is the subject and the person is the one it happened to.
2. **Keep the person in the sentence.** Fully impersonal copy ("an error occurred") loses the acknowledgment that this happened to someone; no-fault is not the same as faceless.
3. **Exception: when the fix requires their action, say so plainly.** Softening is not worth a dead end.
4. **Never use the blameless form for your own failures.** Own those in the first person.

## Apply to your product

- Read your five most-frequent failure strings aloud. How many make the reader the cause?
- Where you removed blame, did the next step survive the rewrite?
- Do your own outages use the same passive construction you use for user mistakes?

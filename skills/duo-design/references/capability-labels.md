# Capability Labels

## Concept

An internal point scale only means something relative to itself. "Level 7" tells the user their position in an ontology they do not have, so they cannot tell whether they are near the thing they actually came for, and they cannot make any claim about themselves anywhere outside your product. A label phrased as a capability — first person, present tense, "I can do X" — states the payoff instead of the position. Anchoring that label to a standard that exists outside your product makes progress *convertible*: it borrows credibility you did not have to build, and it gives the user a reason to continue that outlives their interest in your scoreboard.

## What Duolingo does

Sources: blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-teaches-english (Duolingo blog, 2024-08-07; accessed 2026-09-22) and blog.duolingo.com/new-duolingo-home-screen-design (Duolingo blog, 2022-05-06; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Sections are labelled with first-person capability statements shown directly to the learner, mapped onto an external proficiency framework (CEFR) rather than to internal points. **4 sections** cover the B1 and B2 bands.
- The statements read as outcomes the person can claim rather than as scores — Section 5 is phrased as being able to participate in daily life in the language, and Section 7 as being able to discuss abstract topics such as hopes, goals, and projects. The grammatical subject is the learner, not the curriculum.
- The same rename ran one level down. With the home-screen redesign, unit headers became task-descriptive: **"get directions" replaced "City 3"**, and **"discuss destinations" replaced "Travel 2."** Identical content, a navigation label turned into a motivation surface.
- The section numbers did not disappear — they sit alongside the statements as ordering. The capability text carries the meaning; the number carries the sequence.

**Tension.** Anchoring to an external standard binds you to someone else's definition of progress. It buys legibility and gives up the freedom to define your own levels — and it only works where a standard the user has actually heard of exists. Where none does, a capability label still beats a number, but it is a claim you have to stand behind alone.

## The transferable pattern

Three rules:

1. **Write the label as the user's sentence, not yours.** "I can ship a report to my team" over "Reporting Tier 2." If you cannot finish the sentence honestly, the level may not deliver a capability.
2. **Borrow an external anchor when one exists.** A recognized framework, certification, or job-title vocabulary converts internal progress into something the user can carry elsewhere.
3. **Keep numbers for ordering, not for meaning.** Sequence is real information. It just should not be the only thing the label says.

Anti-patterns:
- Capability statements written aspirationally, describing what the level was meant to teach rather than what a finisher can reliably do.
- Renaming levels without changing the content, so the honest label reveals the tier delivers nothing nameable.
- Anchoring to a standard your audience has never heard of, which is an internal scale with extra syllables.

## Apply to your product

- Take your third progress tier. Can you finish "after this, I can ___" without hedging?
- Is there a standard, certification, or role vocabulary outside your product that your progress could map onto?
- Do your navigation labels describe your content taxonomy or the user's intended task?

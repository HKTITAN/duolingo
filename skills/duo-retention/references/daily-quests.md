# Daily Quests

## Concept

A daily quest is a small goal the product proposes for *today*, refreshed at midnight. "Earn 30 XP." "Complete 2 perfect lessons." "Practice for 10 minutes." The user gets a nudge, an aim, and a finishable target — the inverse of an open-ended app where users have to invent their own reason to act.

Daily quests are the **trigger and frame** layer of the [[habit-loop]]; the streak is the **investment** layer. They work together.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/time-spent-learning-well (Duolingo blog, 2024-06-13; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Daily Quests are "optimized very carefully" for Duolingo's quality metric, **Time Spent Learning Well** — roughly `minutes on path + 0.5 × minutes in other lessons`. The quests are not a generic engagement surface; they are pointed at the sessions that teach.
- **The order matters.** The first quest is the easiest and exists to "get you going." They get harder, and the later ones favour activities that move you forward — finish a unit, read the next Story.
- Duolingo switched the **Monthly Challenge from XP-based to Quest-based** because an XP target let learners bank XP in bulk in the last few days of the month. A quest-based target can only be satisfied by showing up on separate days.
- The paired version, **Friends Quests**, assigns a random challenge to a randomly paired active friend every Tuesday with a five-day window, and supplies pre-written "nudges" so neither party has to compose a message (blog.duolingo.com/friends-quests (Duolingo blog, 2022-09-09; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- Completion produces small rewards (XP, gems, reward chests) that loop back into [[variable-reward]].

## The transferable pattern

Three reasons daily quests outperform "open the app and do whatever":

1. **Cognitive frame.** "Today I need to do X" reduces decision cost. Open-ended apps tax the user's intent every session.
2. **Resolution.** A finished quest is an explicit success moment, separate from "I used the app."
3. **Calibration data.** Quest completion rates tell you which targets are too hard, too easy, or boring — much more legible than session-length data.

Duolingo does not publish a target completion rate, but it does publish the ordering rule, which is the transferable half: **ramp the quests, easiest first.** A rule of thumb that follows from it — aim for a set most engaged users can finish, with the last one genuinely uncertain. If every quest completes, the ladder has no top; if the first one doesn't, it never gets you going.

## Apply to your product

- Could you propose 2–3 small "today" goals to your users each session? What would they be?
- What completion rate would feel achievable but not trivial?
- If you already have a daily-goals system, are the goals tied to the core action, or are they a parallel chore?

# Friction as Stickiness

## Concept

The reflex in product is to remove friction wherever it appears. But friction is the mechanism by which users *invest* in the product. A streak is friction. Hearts are friction. Choosing a learning path is friction. Removing all of it produces a product that's easy to use and easy to forget.

The discipline is: remove friction from the *first session*, add deliberate friction to the *long-term* relationship.

## What Duolingo does

- The first lesson is fast, low-stakes, and immediately rewarding — onboarding friction is minimal.
- Once the user is invested, the product *adds* friction: hearts that limit attempts ([[../duo-gamification/references/hearts-and-energy]]), units that require completion before unlocking, daily quests that require return.
- The streak itself is friction made desirable — the requirement to come back is the feature.

## The transferable pattern

A useful frame:

| Phase | Friction strategy |
|---|---|
| First-time user | Remove every barrier to first value. Frictionlessness wins. |
| Returning user, week 1 | Introduce small commitments — a goal, a streak, a path. |
| Long-term user | Keep meaningful friction; it's the source of sticky behavior. |

Anti-patterns:
- Adding onboarding friction (long signup forms, tutorial walls) — kills first-session retention.
- Removing all friction for long-term users — they have nothing to be invested in.
- Friction as paywall — extracts revenue, doesn't build habit.

## Apply to your product

- Is your first session frictionless? If a user can't reach value in 60 seconds, that's the bug.
- Does your *returning* user have anything they're invested in — a streak, a saved state, a profile, a status?
- Where have you removed friction that was actually doing useful work? (Test: did engagement drop after the change?)

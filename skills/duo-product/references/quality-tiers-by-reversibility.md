# Quality Tiers Keyed to Reversibility

## Concept

A single quality standard is wrong in both directions at once. It taxes high-volume, low-stakes output into a crawl, and it gives an irreversible public artefact the same treatment as something correctable in ten seconds. Tying the tier to **reversibility and audience** makes the right effort level derivable instead of guessed — and it stops the two failure modes teams actually have: over-polishing throwaway work, and under-protecting the thing that cannot be taken back.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/tips-to-improve-writing-skills (Duolingo blog, 2022-03-08; accessed 2026-09-22)

Duolingo publishes **three explicit tiers** for producing written work, each named by its artefact rather than by an abstract quality level:

| Artefact | Audience and reversibility | Instruction |
|---|---|---|
| A message to a friend | One reader, correctable instantly | "Type away and don't worry about mistakes" |
| An email to a colleague | Known reader, awkward to retract | Re-read and edit for clarity and formality |
| A published article | Unbounded readers, effectively permanent | Write several drafts and incorporate feedback from multiple people before publication |

The instruction differs in kind at each tier, not just in intensity: tier one forbids editing, tier two prescribes one pass, tier three prescribes multiple drafts **plus external review**. Register follows the same logic — a clipped fragment like "Will do" is correct in the second tier and wrong in the third.

The tension: tiers are easy to abuse as a blanket excuse to lower the bar. They work only when the top tier stays genuinely expensive and someone can say which tier a given piece of work is in. A tiering scheme where everything lands in tier one is not a scheme, it is a retreat ([[raise-the-bar]]).

## The transferable pattern

1. **Classify by blast radius, not by effort.** Ask who sees it and how hard it is to take back. Those two answers pick the tier.
2. **Make the tiers categorically different.** If tier one is "review a bit less", people will treat all tiers as the same thing and default to the cheapest.
3. **Name the tier at assignment time**, not at review time. The argument about how much polish something deserves is cheap before the work and expensive after it.
4. **Put irreversible artefacts in a tier that mandates outside eyes.** Self-review does not catch the failure modes that matter when nothing can be edited afterwards.

## Apply to your product

- List the last ten things your team shipped externally. How many were one-way doors, and did any of them get a second reviewer?
- What work does your team over-polish because it inherits the standard of a much riskier artefact?
- Could a new hire tell, from your written rules, which tier their next task is in?

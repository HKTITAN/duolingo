# Scope Floor for V1

## Concept

"Minimum viable" is a permission slip: the word *minimum* licenses the gap and the implied V2 absolves it. A scope **floor** is the opposite instrument — a fixed, pre-stated quantity of substance below which you do not launch at all. The floor exists so the first release is worth using; a public commitment to keep working on it is what makes an admittedly partial launch read as a start rather than as abandonment. Ship below the floor and users bounce once and never return; wait for completeness and you delay every lesson real users could have taught you.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-course-creation-at-duolingo (Duolingo blog, 2020-06-11; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The floor is numeric and non-negotiable. A first-version course covers roughly **half of CEFR A1** with a **minimum of 30 skills** and **2,000+ sentences**. Below that line there is no launch.
- The floor is expensive on purpose. Each sentence needs **dozens to hundreds of accepted alternative answers** authored for it, and most contributor teams spent **more than 9 months** reaching the floor.
- The commitment is public and permanent: Duolingo states it **never considers any course truly finished**. The floor is the entry price, not the finish line.
- The vocabulary is policed too. CEO Luis von Ahn had the company **stop saying "MVP"** and call first launches **V1**, tested against one question: if this is the only version that ever exists, are we happy with it? (blog.duolingo.com/product-lessons-friend-streak (Duolingo blog, 2024-09-20; accessed 2026-09-22)) See [[raise-the-bar]].

Two tensions the source states rather than hides. First, the volunteer-contributor model that made hitting the floor affordable also made timelines wildly unpredictable — some teams gave hundreds of hours a month, others stalled for months. Second, that staffing model is **retired**: Duolingo wound down its volunteer contributor programme and moved course creation in-house. The floor is the durable idea; the volunteer pipeline is not.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Express the floor as a countable quantity**, not as a feeling. "Enough to be useful" is unenforceable; "30 units of content, 2,000 examples, every core path complete" survives a schedule argument.
2. **Set the floor before the deadline exists.** A floor negotiated under shipping pressure is just the current state with a number written on it.
3. **Pair the floor with the never-finished promise.** The floor tells users what they get; the promise tells them what happens next. Shipping the floor without the promise reads as a thin launch. Making the promise without the floor reads as an excuse.
4. **Accept the cost.** A real floor means some launches slip and some never happen. That is the mechanism working, not failing.

## Apply to your product

- Write your next release's floor as three numbers. Would you cancel the launch if one of them missed?
- Which of your shipped features would fail the "only version that ever exists" test today?
- Have you ever told users, out loud, that a thing you shipped is not finished — and then kept the promise?

# Difficulty, Paired With Its Mitigation

## Concept

A user who is already intimidated will not believe a claim of ease. They will believe a company that names the exact thing they were afraid of — and then shows the specific mechanism that handles it. The pairing is what matters: a difficulty list on its own is a deterrent, and a mechanism list on its own is marketing. Side by side, the same content becomes a product spec the user can check you against. The same honesty applies to costs users assume are free.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/what-makes-arabic-hard-and-why-that-shouldnt-stop-you-from-learning-it (Duolingo blog, 2026-04-27; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The Arabic landing content opens with a **two-column table**: "Arabic can feel hard at first because…" against "Duolingo's Arabic course deals with this challenge by…". **Five named difficulties, five paired mechanisms** — right-to-left script with position-dependent letter shapes, unwritten short vowels, unfamiliar sounds, gendered second-person forms, and regional variation.
- The table includes the row Duolingo **cannot** fix. The course teaches Modern Standard Arabic, which is openly "not what people grow up speaking" — so no graduate converses in any speaker's home variety. The unmitigated difficulty is printed next to the mitigated ones.
- Hidden costs get stated too. Learners adding a second language are told plainly that splitting a **30-minute** daily session gives less of each, and that they may need to **double total study time** to get the same result per language — because progress tracks absolute exposure, not share of attention (blog.duolingo.com/can-you-learn-two-languages-at-the-same-time (Duolingo blog, 2025-02-05; accessed 2026-09-22)).

The tension worth keeping: publishing the difficulty list costs you some users at the top of the funnel who would otherwise have tried. Duolingo trades that loss for the users who stay because nothing later surprised them.

## The transferable pattern

Build a two-column table for your own domain and hold it to three rules.

| Rule | Why it binds |
|---|---|
| Never list a difficulty without its mechanism | A bare list is a warning label, and users act on warnings |
| Never claim ease without the list | Unearned reassurance is disproved on day two, and takes your credibility with it |
| Print the rows you cannot fix | One honest row makes every other row believable |

Then extend it to arithmetic users get wrong. When people assume two goals cost the same effort as one, the resulting slowdown gets read as your product failing rather than as a tradeoff they chose. Say the multiplier out loud before they hit it.

## Apply to your product

- Write the five hardest things about your domain. Can you name a shipped mechanism next to each, or only a roadmap item?
- Which row of that table is genuinely unfixable — and where would a new user find that out today?
- What does your product silently cost twice as much as users assume, and where could you say so before they commit?

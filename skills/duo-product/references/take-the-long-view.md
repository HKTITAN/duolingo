# Take the Long View

## Concept

The handbook's first principle is the discipline of refusing short-term wins that hurt long-term outcomes. It's the rule that authorizes turning down ad density that lifts revenue, capping notifications that boost open rates, and declining features that pop the metric this quarter and erode it next year.

This is the parent principle the rest of the handbook hangs from. *Raise the Bar*, *Ship It*, and *Show Don't Tell* all interact with the long view; without it, they bend toward expedience.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/product-principles (Duolingo blog, 2024-02-21; accessed 2026-09-22)

Principle #1, stated publicly on the Handbook announcement: *"Take the Long View: If it helps in the short-term but hurts Duolingo in the long-term, it's not right"* (blog.duolingo.com/handbook (Duolingo blog, 2025-02-10; accessed 2026-09-22)).

The VP of Product describes it as the tiebreaker with a named, concrete case:

- **Push notifications vs. the daily streak.** Blanket push would raise DAU quickly; the streak takes far longer to build but creates a habit. *"That's why we invest heavily in the daily streak and don't send tons of push notifications to our users every day."*
- **Three weeks vs. three months.** *"If the best proposal is going to take us three months to build, I want them to choose that idea over the one we could ship in three weeks but is hacky and only OK."*
- **The refused levers are named in the strategy doc**: *"Spammy notifications, deceptive patterns, and heavy paid acquisition are short-term tactics."* Duolingo can decline them because **~80% of users are acquired organically** and about **90% of DAU growth is word of mouth** — the long view is affordable partly because the flywheel already works (blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-company-strategy (Duolingo blog, 2025-04-07; accessed 2026-09-22), blog.duolingo.com/growth-principles (Duolingo blog, 2023-11-03; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- Building a **100-year company** ([[hundred-year-brand]]) is the horizon that makes those refusals legible.

The post is blunt that this does not hold on its own: it calls the principle *"a necessary course correction, because product teams have built-in incentives to do things quickly, to ship fast and score fast wins."* The long view is enforced from above, not assumed from within.

## The transferable pattern

Three rules:

1. **Define the long-term metric.** Without one, "long-term thinking" is a vibe. Pick a horizon (90 days, 1 year, lifetime) and a metric (cohort retention, lifetime value, brand-survey score).
2. **Make every short-term decision name its long-term cost.** Even a "+0" answer is signal — it forces the decision to be conscious.
3. **Build organizational permission to refuse.** The PM who turns down a revenue feature because of a retention model needs cover from leadership. Without it, the long view loses every time.

Tension: cash-runway-constrained companies sometimes *cannot* afford the long view in absolute terms. The discipline is to mark short-term moves as temporary, with a return date.

## Apply to your product

- Name your long-term metric. Is it written down somewhere your team would point to in a heated meeting?
- Find a decision in the last quarter that helped short-term and hurt long-term. Would you make it again?
- Does your team have permission to turn down a revenue lift that costs retention?

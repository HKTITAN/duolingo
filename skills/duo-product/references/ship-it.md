# Ship It

## Concept

Principle #3 is execution discipline. The handbook lists four parts:

- *Optimize "clock speed," minimizing gaps between steps to maintain momentum.*
- *Run hundreds of experiments each week to constantly improve.* (The public figure is per **quarter** — see below.)
- *Ruthlessly prioritize projects with the highest impact and quickly cut what isn't working.*
- *Only introduce a new process if it helps make faster, better decisions.*

The principle is in productive tension with [[raise-the-bar]]: ship fast, but don't ship under the bar. Both are real, both are hard, the friction between them is the work.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/growth-principles (Duolingo blog, 2023-11-03; accessed 2026-09-22)

- **Experiment volume, corrected.** The public figure is **per quarter, not per week**: "at Duolingo we launch hundreds of experiments per quarter" (growth-principles, 2023), and **750+ A/B tests per quarter** in the 2025 strategy post (blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-company-strategy (Duolingo blog, 2025-04-07; accessed 2026-09-22)). Use the quarterly number; the weekly framing overstates it by roughly 13x.
- **Clock speed is justified with arithmetic, not vibes.** Duolingo's worked example: an experiment that lifts retention by 1% and launches **one week earlier** is already adding **7.2% more users per day** by that point, because word-of-mouth compounds on the larger base. ~90% of DAU growth comes from word of mouth, so shipping date is a growth input.
- **Four stated urgency rules**: decide as soon as you have enough data rather than letting experiments sit "collecting more data"; ramp rollout as soon as the change is known safe; sequence by ROI; launch on your largest platform first.
- **Don't pull your punches.** If a feature is imperfect but strictly better than what exists, launch it and iterate — do not hold it back for a tweak and a second experiment.
- **No long-range roadmap at all.** "We don't have a detailed 3- or 5-year strategic plan or product roadmap." The stated method is **The Green Machine**: gather excellent people, give them space to experiment, double down on what works (blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-company-strategy (Duolingo blog, 2025-04-07; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- **What replaces the roadmap is durable direction, not nothing.** Duolingo publishes **five evergreen strategy pillars** — grow users, teach better, grow subscribers, become the proficiency standard, expand beyond language learning — whose *targets* change annually while the pillars themselves do not. Direction comes from the pillars; the route comes from what wins in testing. The whole arrangement is explicitly load-bearing on talent — "achieving our mission depends on attracting and retaining outstanding people," with **>90% annual employee retention** — and without both the retention and the experiment infrastructure, "no roadmap" degrades into drift (duolingo-company-strategy, 2025-04-07).
- Process is a tax: any new ritual must justify itself by improving decisions ([[../duo-culture/references/no-process-without-purpose]]).

The live tension sits inside Duolingo's own words: growth says **"don't pull your punches"** — ship the imperfect improvement. Product says **stop calling it an MVP, call it V1**, and don't ship what isn't ready for learners ([[raise-the-bar]]). Both are real. The reconciliation is the scope of the claim: an *experiment* that is strictly better ships now; a *launched feature* that carries the product's name has to stand on its own if no V2 ever arrives.

## The transferable pattern

Three rules:

1. **Measure clock speed.** What's the typical gap between *idea → decision*, *decision → ship*, *ship → review*? Without naming it, you can't tighten it.
2. **Cut, don't refactor.** Most teams keep failing projects alive longer than the data justifies. Killing is a skill.
3. **Process is debt.** Add a meeting, a review, a step only if it pays for itself in better decisions.

Anti-patterns:
- "Quality vs. speed" framed as a tradeoff. They're orthogonal; the question is which axes you're letting slip.
- Process added by junior managers to feel productive. Process is a cost; it must justify itself.

## Apply to your product

- What's your team's clock speed for a typical change? Could you name the slowest step?
- What's a project that's still alive that the data says should be cut?
- What process did you add this quarter? Did it improve decisions, or just generate artifacts?

# Self-Imposed Stakes

## Concept

A product cannot impose a real penalty on a user without breeding resentment, because the user did not agree to it. But a penalty the user *chose* is a commitment device: failing it reads as their own decision rather than the product punishing them, and the choosing itself is a declaration of intent.

Loss aversion does the rest. A modest amount the user already owns, put at risk, is a stronger motivator than a larger amount offered as a prize — so the wager can be small and still work.

## What Duolingo does

Duolingo offers opt-in stakes at two price points, one in soft currency and one in real money.

- **Streak Challenge.** Learners wager gems on holding a streak for **7, 14 or 30 days**, with **no Streak Freezes permitted** for the duration. The user picks the tier, so they pick their own difficulty, and the freeze ban means they have also voluntarily surrendered the safety valve described in [[power-ups]].
- **Family Plan as accountability group.** Pitched as a plan shared with **up to 5 additional people** — not necessarily family — with the explicit note that having paid makes you more likely to use it. The sunk cost is framed as a feature, and it is one the user volunteered for.
- Source: blog.duolingo.com/tips-for-maintaining-streak (Duolingo blog, 2022-12-19; accessed 2026-09-22)

The tensions worth carrying. First, a wager is a loss mechanic wearing an opt-in label, and the users most attracted to raising their own stakes are frequently the ones already over-invested — the mechanic recruits from the segment that needs it least and can be harmed most. Second, self-imposed stakes explicitly disable the recovery mechanics you built to prevent churn; a user who forfeits a wager has lost both the stake and the streak in the same moment, which is a sharper exit than either alone. Third, the paid version converts a motivation feature into a monetization one, and the honest framing of "you paid, so you will show up" is also a retention argument for a subscription.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Make the stake the user's property.** They must own the thing they risk — earned currency, an earned position, money they chose to spend. A penalty on something you granted them for free is not a stake.
2. **Let them pick the tier.** Multiple durations or amounts mean each user calibrates their own difficulty, which is the difference between a commitment and an imposition.
3. **Keep it strictly opt-in and clearly priced.** The terms — duration, amount, what is disabled — should be legible before they commit, not discovered on failure.
4. **Design the failure landing.** Forfeiting should end in a path back, not a dead end. This is the moment your most invested users decide whether to continue.

## Apply to your product

- What do your users own inside your product that they would voluntarily put at risk?
- If someone wanted to make your product harder on themselves, is there any way to do it today?
- When a self-imposed goal fails, what does your product say — and does it offer the next attempt?

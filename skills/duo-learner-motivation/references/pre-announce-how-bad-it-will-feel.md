# Pre-Announce How Bad It Will Feel

## Concept

A new user has no reference for what normal early progress feels like, so they benchmark against competence and conclude either that the product is broken or that they are not capable. Both conclusions end the same way.

Stating the discomfort in advance, in specific terms, changes nothing about the experience and everything about its meaning. The same clumsy first week that would have read as failure now reads as confirmation they are exactly where the product said they would be. This is the cheapest retention intervention in the set — it is copy.

## What Duolingo does

- Its guide to thinking in a new language sets expectations **before any steps**, as Rule #3. The wording is specific rather than reassuring: you are going to **feel foolish**, you will **not be able to say much**, you will **not sound smart or interesting**, and you are **going to make lots of mistakes** — with the mistakes named as the point rather than as a side effect. It also normalizes translating in your head as simply what beginner and intermediate learners do, rather than a bad habit to be ashamed of. Source: blog.duolingo.com/learn-to-think-in-new-language (Duolingo blog, 2024-05-28; accessed 2026-09-22)
- It pre-announces the mid-stage slowdown the same way, telling self-study users that **"it is normal to encounter a learning slump"** after the rapid beginner phase, and pairing that with instruction to set month-scale concrete goals ("after two months, read a menu") instead of aiming at the unmeasurable headline. Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-to-study-english-on-your-own (Duolingo blog, 2025-08-14; accessed 2026-09-22)
- For absolute beginners it goes further and hands over a taxonomy before they start: **4 language components** (sounds, vocabulary, grammar, conversation rules), **4 skills** (reading, writing, speaking, listening), and **4 named ways things will fail to line up** — non-one-to-one translation, mismatched meaning boundaries, word order, and connector words carrying no meaning at all. Its stated mechanism is that experienced learners "are better prepared to anticipate the kinds of differences that exist", so it front-loads that anticipation for novices. It adds explicit permission for uneven progress: **"It's normal for them to develop at different paces."** Source: blog.duolingo.com/4-things-to-know-about-new-languages (Duolingo blog, 2024-01-09; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **What this does not buy you.** A pre-announcement reattributes a normal curve. It does not rescue a genuinely mis-targeted difficulty — if the content is actually wrong for the user, warning them it will hurt just tells them accurately that it will hurt.

## The transferable pattern

An unlabelled surprise gets attributed to the user's own competence, and that attribution is what produces quitting. A pre-supplied category gives the surprise somewhere to be filed.

1. **Be specific, not encouraging.** "This is hard at first" predicts nothing and confirms nothing. "You will feel foolish, you will not produce much, you will get a lot wrong" is checkable, and being right is what buys the trust.
2. **Say it before the first attempt**, not in a help article the struggling user will never open.
3. **Name the plateau before they hit it.** Early gains are large and visible; the mid-stage slowdown is a property of the curve, and an uninformed user reads it as the product failing or themselves failing.
4. **Hand over a taxonomy of the ways things will be strange.** A named category converts "I'm bad at this" into "this is the expected category-three thing".
5. **Give explicit permission for uneven progress**, because users who are strong on one component and weak on another otherwise average themselves down to the weakest.
6. **Only pre-announce discomfort you intend to keep.** If the difficulty is a defect, fix the calibration instead of narrating it.

## Apply to your product

- Write the honest sentence describing what your product feels like in week one. Is it anywhere in your onboarding, and if not, what is there instead?
- Where on your usage curve does the slowdown happen, and what do you tell users at that point?
- What are the three things about your domain that reliably surprise newcomers — and could a user read all three before their first session?

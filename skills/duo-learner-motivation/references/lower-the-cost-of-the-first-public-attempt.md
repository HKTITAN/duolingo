# Lower The Cost Of The First Public Attempt

## Concept

People withhold participation when they fear being wrong in front of others. The fear is not of the error itself but of the exposure, which means the levers that work are the ones that change what the moment costs socially rather than what it demands technically.

Three of them are cheap and mostly ignored. Pre-expose the material so the live moment is a recall rather than a test. Supply low-content filler so hesitation does not read as incompetence. And ship the undo alongside the change, because perceived reversibility is what gates a risky-feeling try.

## What Duolingo does

- **Pre-exposure.** Duolingo's classroom guidance is to assign a Story for students to read at home *first*, then bring it into class. The stated result is that students **feel safer and are more likely to participate**, because they already understand the material — familiarity converts the live moment from a test into a recall. The same logic underpins repeated reading for fluency. Source: blog.duolingo.com/social-emotional-learning-activities (Duolingo blog, 2022-05-02; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **Filler as a tool.** Duolingo teaches Spanish *muletillas* — "crutches" — explicitly: *o sea, digamos, a ver, bueno, pues*, plus tag questions (*¿cierto?*, *¿sabes?*), framed as instruments for connecting ideas and **buying time**. One guide covers **20+ examples**. The reasoning is social, not linguistic: hesitation reads as incompetence, and the fear of that reading is what stops the attempt. Low-content phrases give the user something correct to say while composing what they actually mean. Source: blog.duolingo.com/spanish-filler-words (Duolingo blog, 2024-10-15; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **Pre-loaded micro-answers.** Duolingo's Korea phrase list includes 당기세요 / 미세요 (pull / push) with the rationale stated outright: **"Yes, you could figure out whether a door wants to be pushed or pulled by simply trying it. But do not underestimate the confidence that comes with knowing this in advance."** The value is confidence at the decision point, not information. Source: blog.duolingo.com/8-phrases-for-korea (Duolingo blog, 2024-05-30; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **The written undo.** Duolingo's post on switching your phone's system language ends with a full section — **"Help, I want to go back to the language I was using before!"** — with annotated iOS and Android screenshots, written so the reader can follow them *while the phone is already in Spanish* ("Find your settings wheel (in Spanish, this is Configuración)"), plus a fallback for users who switched to something else: "try to go off of the visuals." The recovery path is written from inside the changed state, which is the only place it will ever be read. Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-to-change-your-phone-language (Duolingo blog, 2026-04-20; accessed 2026-09-22)

## The transferable pattern

- **Pre-expose before the exposed moment.** Whatever a user will have to perform in front of others, let them meet it privately first. Participation rate is usually the metric you are actually trying to move.
- **Give them something correct to say while they think.** Hesitation is read as incompetence by the person hesitating more than by anyone watching. A stock of safe, low-content moves keeps them in the interaction long enough to succeed.
- **Pre-load the trivial answers that carry social exposure.** Some micro-failures are cheap in outcome and expensive in front of people. Removing the hesitation is worth more than the fact.
- **Ship the undo in the same place as the change**, not in a help centre. Perceived reversibility, not perceived benefit, is what gates a risky try.
- **Write the recovery path for someone already inside the changed state.** Instructions that assume the pre-change context are useless to the only person who needs them.
- **Include a fallback for the case your instructions do not cover.** "If your situation looks different, go off the visuals" is not a cop-out; it is what stops a stuck user from concluding they broke something permanently.

## Apply to your product

- Where does your product ask a user to do something other users will see, and is there any way to rehearse it privately first?
- What does your interface offer a user who is midway through an action and unsure — or does it only offer finish and cancel?
- Take your riskiest-feeling setting change. Is the way back documented next to it, and does that documentation still work once the change has been applied?

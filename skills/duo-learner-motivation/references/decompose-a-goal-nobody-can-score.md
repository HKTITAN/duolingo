# Decompose A Goal Nobody Can Score

## Concept

If your product's headline promise cannot be tested, users never register a win, and permanent non-achievement reads as failure. "Fluent", "proficient", "fit", "data-driven", "organized" are all binaries with no definition — so every state the user actually occupies is on the wrong side of the line.

Decomposing the goal into independently measurable components fixes this in two ways. The user can be strong on some and weak on others, which is both true and motivating, and each component supplies a win the aspirational binary never would.

## What Duolingo does

- It breaks "fluency" into parts and tells users the headline goal is a myth. The decomposition is **four skills** (speaking, writing, reading, listening), **four knowledge areas** (vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, conversation rules), plus **rate of production, accuracy, and comprehensibility** — and the instruction is to track progress on components instead of on the binary. **Tension it concedes:** which components matter depends entirely on what the individual wants to do, so the product cannot pick one scoreboard for everyone. Source: blog.duolingo.com/fluency-language-learning (Duolingo blog, 2022-01-25; accessed 2026-09-22)
- For bands, it adopts CEFR (**A1–C2**) and explains each level through concrete can-do statements rather than scores — including Bong Joon Ho thanking the Oscars in A1 English but switching to B1 Korean for complex thoughts. It then states plainly that **"Most people don't need to master all, or even most, of the levels,"** and that what people mean by "fluent" is usually "comfortable". Its biggest courses (Spanish and French for English speakers) teach **through B2**; others range **A1–B2**. **The cost is real and accepted**: telling users they do not need C1 or C2 caps Duolingo's own upsell into advanced content, in exchange for the goal feeling attainable. Source: blog.duolingo.com/goldilocks-and-the-cefr-levels-which-proficiency-level-is-just-right (Duolingo blog, 2020-02-27; accessed 2026-09-22)
- For the time axis it refuses to promise a duration and publishes a capability map instead — what a user can actually do at each stage, plus honest first-person accounts at **1 month** (early Section 2, beginning of A1), **6 months** (well into Section 3, still A1) and **1 year** of consistent study (end of Section 4, A2). It instructs users to judge by course position rather than elapsed time, and says out loud that **"What takes one person 2 months to learn could take another 6 months."** It also separates the Duolingo Score, a measure of what you can do, from activity metrics like XP. Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-spanish-french (Duolingo blog, 2023-05-10; accessed 2026-09-22)

## The transferable pattern

- **Split the binary into components** the user can be separately good and bad at. A mixed scorecard is honest and produces wins; a single unreachable flag produces neither.
- **Describe each band by what the user can now do**, not by a number or a tier name. A can-do statement is self-evidently achieved or not; a level is unverifiable in the moment.
- **Tell them where to stop.** Explicitly saying most people do not need the top band lets a user locate the level that satisfies their actual purpose. This caps your own upsell — price that against the churn an unreachable goal causes.
- **Publish milestones, not durations.** A time promise you cannot keep converts a slow user into a churned one who believes they failed. Capability checkpoints let them locate themselves honestly.
- **Say pace varies, with a range.** A stated 2-to-6 spread pre-empts the comparison that would otherwise read as personal inadequacy.
- **Separate the capability measure from the activity measure**, and never let activity stand in for capability.
- **Accept that no single scoreboard fits everyone.** Which components matter depends on what the individual came for, so let the user pick which ones they are tracking rather than ranking them for everybody.

## Apply to your product

- Write down the promise on your landing page. Could a user run a test today that returns a yes or no on it — and if not, what are its three components?
- What is the band most of your users actually need, and where do you tell them they can stop there?
- Does your headline number measure what a user can now do, or how much they have used you — and which one do you show them on day 30?

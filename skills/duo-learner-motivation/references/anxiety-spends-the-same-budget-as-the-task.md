# Anxiety Spends The Same Budget As The Task

## Concept

In a domain people find intimidating, the first constraint is affect, not ability. Performing the task requires working memory. Negative emotion occupies the same pool of working memory. So an anxious user executes below their real competence, sees a poor result, and reads it as evidence about themselves rather than about their state.

That is a closed loop: the anxiety produces the bad score, and the bad score produces more anxiety. Lowering the stakes raises measured performance before you improve anything at all about the teaching, the content, or the interface.

## What Duolingo does

- In a survey Duolingo cites, **93% of US adults** reported at least some math anxiety, and it can appear in children as young as **6**. Duolingo also cites research that people with **higher working memory perform better** on math tests — the same resource the anxiety is eating.
- Duolingo Math was built around **low-stakes daily practice** on the research finding that repeated good, low-stakes experiences reduce anxiety. The intervention is the stakes, not the instruction. Duolingo additionally points at deep breathing and brief pre-task expressive writing, both shown to lower anxiety before performance.
- On praise, Duolingo's learning-science position is that people show **lower anxiety and put in more effort** when they are praised for *how they did it and how they can improve*, rather than for inherent ability ("You're really smart!"). It notes that identity-based stereotype threat raises anxiety even in highly accomplished practitioners.
- Source: blog.duolingo.com/math-anxiety (Duolingo blog, 2023-03-06; accessed 2026-09-22)
- Duolingo's hyperpolyglot makes the same point about effort itself — "trying too hard to perfect an accent can give you performance anxiety, which hinders your progress" — and recommends picking a concrete model to imitate, chosen for identification (similar education, gender identity, even sense of fashion), rather than an abstract standard of correctness. Source: blog.duolingo.com/language-tips-from-a-hyperpolyglot (Duolingo blog, 2023-08-30; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **Tension Duolingo states out loud.** Accuracy and comfort "get in the way of each other." Pushing on one costs the other. There is no setting where both are maximized, so the goal has to name which you are optimizing this session.

## The transferable pattern

Treat emotional state as a resource the task competes for, not as a soft concern sitting outside the funnel.

1. **Measure the stakes, not just the difficulty.** Two screens with identical cognitive load produce different outcomes if one of them scores you.
2. **Default the recurring surface to low stakes.** Frequent, cheap, unscored repetition is the intervention that moves anxiety. A single well-designed high-stakes moment does not.
3. **Never praise the trait.** Every piece of feedback the system emits should point at the action taken or the next action available. Trait praise installs a fixed frame, and under a fixed frame the cheapest way to protect yourself is to stop attempting.
4. **Watch for identity threat.** If a user belongs to a group your domain stereotypes as bad at it, their anxiety is higher at equal skill, and your copy is either adding to that or subtracting from it.
5. **Name which you are optimizing.** Comfort and correctness trade against each other. A surface that pretends to maximize both ships neither.

## Apply to your product

- Which screen in your product is the one users describe as making them nervous, and what does it score, count, compare, or expose?
- Read every automated message your system sends. How many praise or blame the person rather than the action?
- If you deliberately halved the stakes of your most-used surface for a week, what would you expect to happen to completion rate — and have you ever tested it?

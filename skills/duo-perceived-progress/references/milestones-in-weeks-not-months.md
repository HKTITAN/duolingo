# Milestones in Weeks, Not Months

## Concept

A goal with no agreed definition cannot be reached, so it never produces a completion event — and completion events are the thing actually in short supply. Worse, an undefined target is unfalsifiable in the wrong direction. Because nobody can say when it has been met, every genuine gain gets filed as "still not there", and effort accumulates as evidence of inadequacy instead of evidence of progress.

The repair has two parts. Move the next finish line to a few weeks out, so the reward arrives on a schedule short enough to sustain effort. And make it specific enough that the user can observe daily movement toward it and know unambiguously when it is done. A dated, checkable target also doubles as a scheduled moment to change approach before momentum is gone.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/sticking-with-it-tips-for-staying-motivated (Duolingo blog, 2026-06-04; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo's stated guidance is to **set deadlines a few weeks out**, and to treat each deadline as a checkpoint — look at what is working, change what is not — rather than only as a pass/fail moment.
- It tells learners to **replace "fluent" with concrete targets**, such as understanding one specific song or one specific film, with **plenty of smaller goals in between**. The unit of ambition is an artifact you can point at, not an adjective.
- The reasoning against the vague goal is spelled out elsewhere — fluency is dropped as a goal on the grounds that it is vague and its usual definitions are too strict to describe what beginner or intermediate ability actually delivers, with tangible milestones and small celebrated achievements recommended in its place (blog.duolingo.com/critical-period-learning (Duolingo blog, 2026-04-14; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The same move is the **first** of the five prescriptions for the intermediate slump. "I want to get better" is rejected outright in favour of goals shaped like a task — read and understand a news article about the economy, follow a recipe video and cook the meal — and the post closes by handing goal-setting to the user, telling them to take control of their own learning (blog.duolingo.com/intermediate-plateau (Duolingo blog, 2024-02-27; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**The tension.** Handing target-setting to the user is a real transfer of control. Past a certain point no single prescribed sequence fits everyone, so the curriculum starts feeling arbitrary and the user-chosen goal is the only relevant one — but it is also a goal you did not design, cannot schedule, and may not be able to support well.

## The transferable pattern

- The scoring test comes before the motivating test. If two reasonable people could disagree about whether the goal was met, it is not a goal — it is a mood.
- Weeks, not months. The interval between finish lines is the interval over which someone can decide this is not working.
- Between the big targets, seed **smaller ones the user cannot miss hitting**. Frequency is what sustains effort; size is what makes it memorable. You need both, and frequency is the scarcer of the two.
- Make each deadline a **review point with a named decision** — continue, adjust, or abandon. A deadline that only reports failure teaches people to stop setting them.
- Once your prescribed sequence stops fitting, shift from setting the target to helping the user state one, and then supporting it. Do that deliberately rather than letting the path quietly become irrelevant.

## Apply to your product

- Write down the goal your product implicitly asks users to hold. Could an outsider tell from your data whether a given user has met it?
- What is the shortest interval in which a new user can complete something nameable, and what is that interval for a two-year user?
- At what point does your prescribed sequence stop matching what individual users need, and what do you offer them at exactly that point?

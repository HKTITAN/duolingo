# A Portable Number in Can-Do Terms

## Concept

Internal progress is worthless outside the product that issued it. Levels, points, badges and unit counts mean something to you and nothing to the user's manager, their client or their next employer, so the effort behind them cannot be spent anywhere. A single scalar that maps to a recognised external standard, and that is documented in plain statements of what the holder can now do, converts that effort into a portable claim. It motivates for a different reason than a points balance does, and it markets, because every place the user posts it is a referral you did not pay for.

The trap is that a number built to move every session will keep moving through a stage where capability is consolidating rather than expanding. Say so out loud, or the user concludes the number is lying.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/what-does-duolingo-score-60-mean (Duolingo blog, 2026-03-05; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo extended the **Duolingo Score** to all language courses as a single "simple, clear metric", mapped onto CEFR bands — **59 corresponds to A2**, and **60 marks the transition into independent use**.
- The score is documented in **can-do terms** rather than curriculum terms. At 60 the published claims include linking clauses with words like because, but, so, when and if; getting around transport and restaurants; and giving a short presentation. That is a description the user can check against their own experience, and repeat to someone else.
- Duolingo **partnered with LinkedIn** so learners can display the Score on their profile — the number leaves the product and lands somewhere it has social value.
- The surrounding expansion is the reason a common scale was needed at all — **172 new courses launched in 2025**, putting **8 major languages in front of speakers of 28 languages**, the largest content expansion in the company's history. Without a shared scalar, progress in one course could not be compared with progress in another.
- **The divergence is acknowledged in the same post.** Reaching 60 "doesn't mean everything is easy" — Duolingo says the stage feels challenging in new ways. Progress there is consolidation, not new capability, so a rising number and a stalling felt sense pull apart.
- The companion post on the intermediate slump names **five causes**, and cause four is that other people can already understand you — a good problem that stalls improvement because it becomes hard to know what to work on. The prescribed fix is to seek a finer-grained signal than "did it work" (blog.duolingo.com/intermediate-plateau (Duolingo blog, 2024-02-27; accessed 2026-09-22)).

## The transferable pattern

- Pick an **external standard that already exists** in your domain and anchor your scalar to it, rather than inventing a private scale nobody can interpret. Borrowed legitimacy is cheaper than earned legitimacy.
- Write each level as **what the holder can now do**, in the user's own task language. A definition stated in your internal units explains nothing.
- Make it **exportable on purpose** — a profile, a certificate, a shareable card. The export is the distribution channel, so design it as one.
- Name the stage where the number outruns the felt experience, before the user notices it themselves. Being told "this stage feels flat and here is why" is very different from silently suspecting the metric is inflated.
- One number. The moment you have three, the user has to decide which one counts, and a metric they have to arbitrate is not a motivator.

## Apply to your product

- Is there an external standard, certification or shared vocabulary in your market you could map your progress onto instead of inventing one?
- Write your top three levels as sentences beginning "I can" — if you cannot finish them without naming your own features, the level is not portable.
- Where in your curve does your number keep rising while the user's felt capability is flat, and what do you currently say to them at that point?

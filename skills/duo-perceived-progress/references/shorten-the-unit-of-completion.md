# Shorten the Unit of Completion

## Concept

The reward that sustains effort is the discrete act of finishing something. It arrives on a schedule, and that schedule is set by one variable you control directly — how long your unit of work is. A long unit ending in one large celebration feels like trudging for most of its length, because for most of its length nothing has been completed.

Re-chunking is unusually cheap. The content does not change, the difficulty does not change, and nothing new has to be produced. You are changing where the boundaries fall, and with them the frequency of the reinforcement and the perceived cost of starting. Both matter most in the middle of the lifecycle, where units naturally get denser and the visible next step gets more intimidating exactly as felt progress slows.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/intermediate-mini-units (Duolingo blog, 2026-03-04; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo restructured **all intermediate content from long units into mini-units**, completing the rollout by **March 2026**. Each mini-unit introduces a handful of new words and then immediately applies them in Stories, DuoRadio and Video Call rather than drilling them in isolation.
- The band it targets is precise — learners with a **Duolingo Score between 60 and 130**, which is the intermediate stretch where the plateau lives. The company says it is now testing the same approach elsewhere in the course.
- The stated trigger was learner research, not a metrics hunch — long units "could feel repetitive and abstract", and learners wanted more chances to use the material in realistic situations.
- **Both motives are named honestly**, and the second is doing at least as much work as the first. Alongside the learning argument, the post says plainly that it is now more satisfying to complete a node and move on to the next one.
- The earlier version of the same move appears in the 2025 year-in-review — for learners above Score 60, units were broken into **smaller chunks described as more engaging and less intimidating, while keeping the same interactive exercises** (blog.duolingo.com/product-highlights (Duolingo blog, 2025-12-10; accessed 2026-09-22)). Same content, different boundaries.

**The tension.** Cut too far and completion stops meaning anything — a finish line every ninety seconds trains users to expect a reward for trivial effort, and the celebration you attach to it loses its value. The gain here came from re-chunking dense material for one identified segment, not from shrinking everything for everyone.

## The transferable pattern

- Measure your **time-to-first-completion** and your median gap between completions. If either is measured in days, that is the number to attack before you build anything new.
- Re-chunking is a boundary change, not a content change. It is one of the few motivation levers that costs authoring time rather than authoring work.
- Apply it **where the density rises**, not uniformly. Most products have one stretch — onboarding-to-competent, or free-to-serious — where units get heavy and felt progress flattens at the same time. That is where the smaller chunk pays.
- Put a real use of the material at the end of each chunk instead of stacking practice and deferring application. The completion event and the point of the work then coincide.
- Keep a floor. When you halve a unit, check that the completion moment still carries weight — if the reward stops landing, you have traded frequency for meaning.

## Apply to your product

- What is your smallest unit a user can finish and feel done with, and how long does it take a real user, not you?
- Where in your product does the work get denser without the milestones getting closer together — and what would those units look like cut in half?
- If you shortened your units tomorrow, what would still be worth celebrating at the boundary?

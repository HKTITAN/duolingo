# Publish the Honest Timeline and a Ladder

## Concept

Whatever timeline your category advertises becomes the reference point your users judge themselves against. If the ambient promise is "results in six weeks", a user at month five with genuine, normal progress experiences a deficit — not because they failed, but because they were handed the wrong yardstick. They churn believing the problem is them.

Correcting this costs you nothing in truth and a lot in nerve, because you are contradicting the promise your competitors' ads are making. It only works if you do two things at once. State the real timeline, and publish a standard ladder so the user can locate themselves on it. That converts "am I behind?", which has no answer and therefore festers, into "I am at stage two of six", which does.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/can-you-learn-a-language-in-6-months (Duolingo blog, 2026-08-18; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo published the flat statement that you cannot learn a language in six months, then mapped **six CEFR levels across three bands** — A1/A2 beginner, B1/B2 intermediate, C1/C2 advanced — onto what is realistic inside a **6-month window**. A1 almost certainly; A2 with enough time; B1 tricky without immersion; B2 and C1 highly unlikely; C2 difficult even in your first language, and something many people never reach.
- The bad news is reframed as **permission rather than hedged**. The message is not "it depends" — it is that the learner is not falling behind, because the schedule they were measuring against was never real.
- The same move appears in the myths post, which refuses the quick-fix framing outright — no easy hacks, no whole language in six weeks — and **immediately pairs the refusal with near-term personal goals** so the user has something visibly moving this week (blog.duolingo.com/language-learning-myths (Duolingo blog, 2023-03-23; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- **The top of the ladder is not the definition of success.** Duolingo published learners whose stated goals were explicitly not mastery — resettlement caseworkers who wanted only to recognise basic words and manage light conversation with clients, describing it as a respect thing rather than a fluency goal, and a student who valued her course precisely because she was not pushing toward one. Streaks quoted in those interviews are **53 days and 100 days**, not multi-year (blog.duolingo.com/ukrainian-learner-stories (Duolingo blog, 2022-11-11; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**The tensions, both real.** This is a growth-hostile message from a growth-driven company, contradicting its own category's marketing. And supporting the modest goal cuts against depth monetization — any product whose revenue scales with users pursuing mastery has a structural incentive to make the small goal feel insufficient.

## The transferable pattern

- Find out what timeline your users believe, then check it against your own cohort data. The gap between those two numbers is a churn generator you are currently funding.
- Publish the real one **with a ladder**, not alone. Honesty without a locating device just reads as bad news, and a user who cannot place themselves has no way to convert your candour into reassurance.
- Pair every honest long-horizon statement with **something visible this week**. The truth about month eighteen is only survivable next to evidence about Tuesday.
- Do not set your win condition at the top of the ladder. If mastery is the only success state, the large majority of your users are failures by your own metric, and they can feel it. Many of them chose a bounded outcome deliberately and will stay for years if you let that count.
- Watch the incentive pulling the other way. If your pricing rewards depth, someone will eventually argue for making the modest goal feel incomplete. Name that tradeoff before it gets made quietly.

## Apply to your product

- What does your marketing imply about time-to-value, and what does your data actually say? Who is living in the gap right now?
- Is there a recognised ladder in your domain you could publish so a user can answer "where am I?" without asking you?
- Which of your users deliberately want a small bounded outcome, and does anything in your product currently tell them that is not enough?

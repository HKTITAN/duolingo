# Scaffolding Belongs on a User-Controlled Switch

## Concept

Every support you add has two populations. For the person who would otherwise be stuck, it is the thing that keeps them in the product. For the person who is ready to work without it, it is a crutch that quietly prevents them from building the underlying capability — they lean on the aid and never do the harder retrieval the task was supposed to demand.

A global default cannot serve both. On by default patronises the capable and caps their ceiling; off by default strands the struggling; omitting the aid altogether is the same as off with extra steps. The switch is the answer, and not because you are ducking the decision — because where a given user's boundary sits is information you cannot infer for them, and it moves as they improve. Put the control in their hands, in the flow, and let them move it.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/korean-course-updates (Duolingo blog, 2026-07-07; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Transliterations were added for **every** Korean sentence — and put behind a **gear icon the learner can toggle mid-lesson**, rather than always shown or never shown. The aid exists for everyone; the decision about needing it belongs to the learner, at the moment they hit the sentence.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/improving-how-duolingo-teaches-chinese-and-other-languages (Duolingo blog, 2020-02-03; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Non-Latin-script courses answered in a **word bank** so learners were not forced to install an unfamiliar keyboard. That removed a real precondition, but word banks require far less active recall than typing — the accommodation had silently lowered the ceiling for everyone.
- Duolingo's fix was an **input-mode toggle** letting learners opt into keyboard entry, first for those courses and then extended to all courses. It is available **at level 2 and above** — the scaffolding is unconditional at the very start, and becomes a choice once the user has enough footing to make one.

## The transferable pattern

When you find yourself arguing about whether to show a hint, a simplification, a preview, an autocomplete or a guided mode, the argument is usually unresolvable because both sides are right about different users. Convert it into a control. Three properties make the control work rather than decorate:

1. **It is reachable where the difficulty is**, not buried in a settings page three screens away from the moment of need.
2. **It is reversible and cheap to flip**, so a user can test their own boundary instead of committing to an identity.
3. **The default is chosen deliberately for the harder-off population**, because they are the ones who will not go looking.

Watch the second-order effect the toggle exposes: once the harder mode exists, you learn what fraction of users actually want depth, which is a fact you cannot get from a product that only ships one mode.

## Apply to your product

- Name one aid your product shows everybody. Who does it hold back, and what would they gain from turning it off?
- Where would the control live so a user reaches it at the moment they need it rather than after they have already failed?
- Is your default set for the strongest users or the weakest? Say which, and say why, out loud.

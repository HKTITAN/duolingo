# A Slow, Decomposed Version Is Not the Answer Key

## Concept

Teams resist offering a slower or broken-apart version of a timed stimulus because it feels like giving away the answer. Usually it is not. A large share of failures on fast-moving material are *perceptual segmentation* failures rather than knowledge failures: the person knows every part, but cannot find the boundaries between the parts at the speed the material arrives. Slowing it down and exposing the seams restores the boundaries. It does not supply the content.

That distinction is the whole test. An aid that hands over what the user was supposed to produce is an answer key. An aid that only makes the input parseable — slower, segmented, repeatable — turns a wall into a ramp while leaving the task fully intact.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/learning-with-hearing-aids (Duolingo blog, 2026-01-20; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The **turtle button** replays the sentence more slowly, with pauses inserted between words — the seams made visible rather than the answer supplied.
- The speaker button can be tapped an **unlimited** number of times. No rationing, no penalty for repeats.
- Users can replay **after entering an answer and before submitting**, which is the moment a person actually wants to re-check themselves.
- For "Repeat what you hear", a **Reveal** shows the text while the character says the line again.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/black-history-french-podcast (Duolingo blog, 2021-07-14; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Pacing control also solves the case where one artifact must serve two incompatible consumption speeds. For the Duolingo French Podcast "Paris noir" walking-tour episode — launched **14 July 2021**, **five stops** — a **chime** was added to give in-person listeners time to walk between locations, while at-home listeners consume continuously. A Google Map with **timestamps matching the audio** shipped alongside as a synchronised companion, and a producer was sent to Paris to capture ambient sound and confirm the walking directions were geographically accurate.
- The instructive part is that Duolingo did not cut a separate edit per audience. One artifact plus an explicit pacing cue and a sync affordance covered both.

## The transferable pattern

Next to any timed, fast-moving or dense stimulus, ship a decomposed version: slower, with the joins exposed, repeatable without limit and without cost. Check it against the answer-key test before shipping — does it reveal the structure of the input, or does it reveal the output you were asking the person to produce? Only the second is cheating.

Repeat limits deserve specific suspicion. They are usually there to stop a theoretical abuse nobody has committed, and their real effect is to make a hesitant user ration their own attempts. And where one artifact has to serve two audiences whose difference is *timing*, an explicit pacing cue plus a synchronised companion piece is cheaper and more honest than picking a winner or maintaining two edits.

## Apply to your product

- Where in your product does something move at a fixed speed the user cannot change? What is your evidence that speed is part of what you are measuring?
- Do you cap retries, replays or re-reads anywhere? Write down the abuse that cap prevents, then estimate how many hesitant users it quietly taxes.
- If two groups consume the same artifact at different speeds, are you planning two versions — and could one pacing affordance replace both?

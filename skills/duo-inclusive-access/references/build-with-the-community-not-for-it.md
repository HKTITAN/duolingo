# Build With the Community, Not For It

## Concept

Outsiders building for a group make two predictable errors. They sanitise — cutting the uncomfortable material that the actual users most need — and they canonicalise, quietly ruling which of several legitimate variants is the real one. Both errors come from the same place: internal review can catch inaccuracy, but it cannot supply the judgement about what is *needed* or what a choice will *read as* to the people it describes.

The fix is structural rather than attitudinal. Put named people from the community in authoring and deciding roles, let them scope the content including the parts that make you nervous, and when a genuinely contested choice has to be made anyway, ship it as a stated choice rather than as a truth. That last move does not make the decision uncontested. It makes it legible, which is the most you can honestly claim.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/haitian-creole-duolingo (Duolingo blog, 2022-02-22; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The Haitian Creole course — Duolingo's **41st language**, serving roughly **12 million speakers** in Haiti and the diaspora — was built by professors and students led by Nick André and Nyya Flores Toussaint, organised on the Haitian co-op model of a *konbit*, with contributors named individually in the post.
- The community authors deliberately included sentences and lessons addressing historical and current social justice issues, because those are the political, social and spiritual conversations learners would actually have. An outside team would have cut exactly that material.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/oral-vs-written-languages (Duolingo blog, 2022-11-29; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Haitian Creole has been written since at least the **late 1700s** and Haitians write it differently by dialect, accent and personal innovation. Duolingo deferred the orthographic decisions to community language experts for the course launched in **February 2022**.
- Tension the post states plainly: the team's consensus does not mean all Haitian Creole speakers agree with the choices. The process reduces the harm of the decision; it does not make the decision uncontested. (The Yiddish course hit the adjacent version of this — the team had to record all audio themselves for lack of existing resources.)

Source: blog.duolingo.com/inclusion-matters-here (Duolingo blog, 2020-06-30; accessed 2026-09-22)

- A Duolingo creative producer convened LGBTQIA+-identifying employees to review Duolingo Stories storylines. One suggestion was a coming-out plot; a colleague reframed it — representation should also mean a character just doing something ordinary who happens to be queer. When identity is the premise, the content teaches that such people are a topic; when it is a background fact, it teaches that they are unremarkable.

## The transferable pattern

Three rules, in descending order of how much they cost you:

1. **Authorship beats review.** Consultation lets you keep the pen, which means you keep the sanitising instinct. Put people from the group in the role that decides what goes in.
2. **Represent incidentally.** A person doing something ordinary who happens to belong to a group normalises; a story whose entire plot is that identity marks it as exceptional. You will usually not see the difference from outside, which is itself the argument for rule 1.
3. **Ship contested choices as choices.** Where you must canonicalise something with no single right answer, say who decided, on what basis, and that disagreement exists. Presenting it as the correct form is a claim about everyone who does it differently.

## Apply to your product

- Which of your users belong to a group nobody on the team belongs to? Who decides what content or defaults they get — and are they in the room with a pen or with a comment box?
- Where has your product canonicalised something contested (a format, a name, a category, a default)? Could you name the decision-maker and the basis publicly without embarrassment?
- Look at how your under-represented users appear in your content. Are they the subject, or are they present doing something ordinary?

# Give the Free Tier an Owner With Standing

## Concept

A principle with no owner is a preference, and preferences lose to quarterly targets. "We do not paywall the core" holds for about as long as no one has a revenue miss. What makes it structural is that some function inside the company, one that is not measured on revenue, has real influence over product decisions — and that leadership treats that function's metric as first-order rather than as a constraint to be traded against. Without that, the commitment erodes one small reclassification at a time, and nobody can point at the meeting where it was abandoned.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/why-i-interned-at-duolingo-rebecca-hu-product-management-intern (Duolingo blog, 2022-08-26; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Learning content is **never paywalled**. Super sells unlimited hearts, extra Practice Hub lesson types and ad removal; Max sells AI features (Explain My Answer, Roleplay, Video Call). **40+ languages and 100+ courses** are free, with **two paid tiers** sitting on top.
- The structural piece — an entire **Learning and Curriculum team heavily influences product decisions**, and leadership treats **learner impact as a first-order metric** rather than as a check on revenue decisions made elsewhere.
- The commitment is visible enough to act as a recruiting asset. An engineer cites the refusal to charge for content, **even when it makes monetization harder**, as her reason for joining full-time (blog.duolingo.com/why-i-chose-duolingo-margarita-misirpashayeva (Duolingo blog, 2021-02-03; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- **The tension is real and Duolingo pays it visibly.** The same constraint forces the company to **publicly decline most requested new languages**, redirecting resources into deepening existing courses instead. A commitment that never costs you a popular decision is not a commitment — it is marketing.
- Detail that ages — the hearts economy named here has since been replaced by energy. The governance arrangement is the durable part; the feature list is not.

## The transferable pattern

Three things have to be true for a free-core commitment to survive.

1. **A named function owns it.** Not a value on a wall and not a founder's memory. A team with headcount whose job is the non-revenue outcome.
2. **That function has standing in product decisions.** Not review rights, not a late veto, but influence early enough that proposals arrive already shaped by it.
3. **Its metric is reported alongside revenue, at the same altitude.** A metric that appears only in its own team's deck will lose every argument it enters.

And one test — the commitment must visibly cost you something popular. If you cannot name a decision you declined because of it, the commitment is not binding on anyone, and you should stop citing it as a differentiator.

## Apply to your product

- Who in your company can stop a revenue-positive change that degrades the free experience, and have they ever done it?
- Which non-revenue number appears in the same review as revenue, at the same level of seniority? If none does, that is the gap.
- Name one popular request you have declined because of your free-tier commitment. If you cannot, what would the first one be?

# A Paying Minority Funds a Complete Free Tier

## Concept

Most freemium products are built backwards. The free tier is designed as a demo — functional enough to interest you, broken enough to annoy you into paying. That design makes the free tier a bad advertisement, because the thing most people experience is the crippled version, and that is the version they describe to other people. The alternative is to make the free tier genuinely complete and sell convenience and amplification on top. A small fraction of users with high willingness to pay funds the rest, and the large free population is precisely what makes the paid features worth having.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/is-duolingo-free (Duolingo blog, 2024-09-23; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Every course is free — **40+ language courses, plus math and music**. Duolingo states plainly that a small number of paying users is what makes the courses free for everyone else.
- Revenue comes from three places stacked on one free core — in-app **ads**, **Super Duolingo** (ad-free plus gamification perks), and **Duolingo Max** (GPT-4-powered roleplay and tailored grammar explanations).
- Duolingo states that the free version is **just as effective** as a subscription — the claim is about outcome parity, not feature parity (blog.duolingo.com/why-i-chose-duolingo-margarita-misirpashayeva (Duolingo blog, 2021-02-03; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- Reach at the top of the funnel is treated as an asset in its own right. **Constructed languages — Esperanto, High Valyrian, Klingon — consume only a small share of marketing and partnership resources** but widen the entry point and help subsidize the serious courses.
- **The tension.** Refusing to gate the core means the paid tier must sell something genuinely optional, which caps revenue per user and makes the whole model dependent on scale. A product that cannot reach scale cannot run this play — the arithmetic only closes when the free base is very large.

## The transferable pattern

Say out loud which of these two free tiers you are running, because they need opposite designs.

- **A demo.** Deliberately incomplete. Judged on conversion rate. Every improvement to it is a cost, so it decays.
- **A complete tier.** Delivers the real outcome. Judged on reach and on the paid tier's attach rate. Improvements to it compound, because they enlarge the population the paid tier sells into.

If you choose the second, the test is whether a free user can finish the job and be genuinely satisfied. If the honest answer is "yes, but slowly and with ads," you are running the complete tier. If it is "no, they hit a wall," you are running a demo and should stop describing it as generous.

The failure mode is running a demo while telling yourself the second story. It shows up as an ever-growing list of features that were core last year and are "premium" this year.

## Apply to your product

- Can a free user complete the core job end to end today, without paying? If not, at which step do they stop, and was that step always paid?
- What fraction of your users pay, and what would the free tier need to look like for that fraction to be smaller but the total revenue larger?
- Which parts of your free surface are deliberately worse than they could be, purely to create upgrade pressure? What does that cost you in referrals?

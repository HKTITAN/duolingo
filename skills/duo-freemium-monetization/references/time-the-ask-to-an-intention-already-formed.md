# Time the Ask to an Intention Already Formed

## Concept

A conversion ask has to do two jobs — make the user want the outcome, and make them accept the price. The first job is by far the expensive one, and most of your conversion budget is spent on it badly. There are moments when a population independently resolves to acquire a capability, for reasons that have nothing to do with you. If your offer lands inside one of those moments, the wanting is already done and your offer only has to clear the price objection. You have not persuaded anyone; you have shown up as the instrument of a decision they already made, which is the cheapest thing you can possibly be.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/engineering-new-year-promotion (Duolingo blog, 2022-12-29; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo runs a **New Year's Promotion** — a limited-time discount on Super Duolingo — timed to the New Year holiday, stated explicitly as supporting learners' resolutions rather than as a generic seasonal sale.
- It ships across **all three surfaces — Android, iOS and Web** — because a moment-timed campaign that only exists on one platform misses the users who form the intention elsewhere.
- **Several A/B tests are layered on top** of the promotion, so the moment is treated as a recurring experiment surface rather than as a one-off marketing push. The timing is the constant; the treatment is what gets learned.
- The engineering framing matters — a date-bounded promotion across three clients is a delivery problem with a hard deadline and no option to slip, which is why Duolingo writes about it as engineering impact rather than as campaign copy.
- **Two tensions worth naming.** A reliably annual discount teaches users to wait for it, which suppresses conversion in the other eleven months — the discount's true cost includes that deferred demand. And concentrating a promotion into a fixed window concentrates the operational risk too; there is no rolling back into next week.

## The transferable pattern

Look for moments where the intention forms outside your product, then meet them.

- **Calendar moments** — a date when a whole population independently resolves something. Cheap to find, but everyone else has found them too, so you compete on offer rather than on insight.
- **Lifecycle moments** — a change in the user's circumstances that creates the need. Harder to detect, far less contested, and usually a better fit.
- **In-product moments** — the user has just hit the limit, just finished something, just failed at something the paid tier would have fixed. The strongest signal you get, and the only one you fully control.

Match the moment to the offer's shape. A calendar moment suits a discount, because the intention is already there and price is the last obstacle. An in-product moment suits an explanation rather than a discount, because the user has just experienced the specific gap and needs to know that you close it. Discounting at an in-product moment wastes margin on someone who was already convinced.

## Apply to your product

- What happens outside your product that makes someone decide they need what you do? Can you detect it, or only guess at it?
- When does your current upgrade ask fire — at a moment of user intention, or at a moment of company convenience like a quarter end?
- If you run a recurring seasonal discount, how much of its conversion is demand that simply waited for it?

# Raise the Ceiling for Users Who Finished You

## Concept

Every growth conversation is about acquiring users who have not arrived. There is a second lever, usually invisible, pointing the other way — the users who have consumed everything you offer and have nowhere left to go. They churn at the exact moment their skill, their habit and their sunk investment all peak, which is the moment they were most likely to convert, renew, or recommend you. Extending the ceiling retains a population with an acquisition cost of zero and a demonstrated willingness to invest. It is expensive to build for, because the content is advanced and the cohort is small, and it is still often the cheapest retained user you will find.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/courses-teach-advanced-content (Duolingo blog, 2026-04-22; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo extended **nine of its most popular courses** — English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Japanese, Korean and Chinese — from **A2 to B2** on the CEFR scale.
- In its own measurement units, that moved the ceiling from a **Duolingo Score of 59 (A2) to 129 (B2)**. **B2 corresponds to Scores 100 to 129** and is the **fourth of six CEFR levels**.
- The target level was chosen for its external consequence, stated explicitly — B2 is the proficiency that lets you **get a job or attend university** in that language. The ceiling was set at a threshold the user already cares about, not at an arbitrary next tier.
- Raising the number was the smaller half of the work. The new range was **backfilled with content** — advanced Stories, DuoRadio episodes, Explain My Answer coverage, and mini-units — so that the extension was a real place to go rather than a relabelled dead end.
- **The tension.** This was done for **nine courses, not the 100+** that exist. Ceiling extension is content-production cost spent on the smallest cohort you have, so it can only be funded where the population is large enough to repay it. That is a real limit, not an oversight, and it means most of your long tail will stay capped.

## The transferable pattern

Find your exhaustion point, then decide whether to extend it.

**Finding it.** Look for a cohort whose usage is high and stable and then stops without a triggering event — no cancellation complaint, no failure, no competitor. That silence is the signature of running out of product.

**Extending it well** takes three things. A **ceiling defined by an outcome the user already values**, not by your internal progression scheme, so that reaching it is legible as an achievement. **Real depth behind the new range**, because an extended ceiling with thin content churns the same cohort a second time and with less goodwill. And an **honest scope decision** — extend where the population repays the production cost, and say plainly that you are not extending elsewhere.

## Apply to your product

- Where does your most engaged cohort run out? Can you see that moment in your data today, or does it look like ordinary churn?
- What external outcome sits just past your current ceiling that a user would already recognize as worth reaching?
- If you raised the ceiling, what would have to exist in the new range for it to be a destination rather than a label?

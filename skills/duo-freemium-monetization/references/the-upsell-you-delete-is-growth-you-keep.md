# The Upsell You Delete Is Growth You Keep

## Concept

An upgrade screen is not neutral. It is a piece of interface that every new user must interpret, and interpretation depends on whether they have a model for what it is asking. A user who has never held a recurring subscription does not read "start free trial" as an optional offer. They read a prominent button on a screen that blocks the thing they came for, and they conclude the product is closed to them. You lose the user and collect nothing, because a cohort that does not understand recurring billing was never going to convert anyway. The trade is churn for zero.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/language-learning-for-the-next-billion-duolingo-in-india (Duolingo blog, 2020-09-24; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Newly-online users were observed navigating by **visual salience** — tapping the most prominent element on a screen without parsing it. A prominent subscribe button therefore gets tapped, gets read as a hard paywall, and the user leaves believing the product is not for them.
- Duolingo removed the early-lifecycle Duolingo Plus promotion for new learners in India. Result — **+1% DAU**.
- Duolingo then removed Duolingo Plus **entirely** in India and several other Asian countries. Result — **+6% DAU**.
- Interviewed users said they would prefer a **one-time fee** over a recurring subscription, which is a statement about the billing model, not about willingness to pay.
- Context for the size of the bet — new India learners were up **400% year over year**, against **500M+ Indians coming online for the first time between 2017 and 2022**, driven by mobile data at roughly **1.5GB per day for $6 per month**.
- **The tension is explicit and expensive.** This is forgoing subscription revenue in the fastest-growing market on the thesis of "grow here first, monetize later." It only pays off if a monetization model that fits the market is found later. Duolingo states the bet plainly rather than presenting it as a free win.

## The transferable pattern

Treat the upgrade surface as a segment-specific decision, not a global one. The same screen has different expected value in different populations, and in some it is negative.

Run the test in this order. First, find the segments where conversion is near zero — not low, near zero. Second, check whether the upgrade surface is being *seen* by those users early, before they have experienced the core value. Third, remove it for that segment and measure active users, not revenue, because the revenue you are giving up is already approximately nothing.

Two things make this hard to do. The upsell is usually owned by a team measured on revenue, so no one is incentivized to delete it; and the growth it buys shows up in a different dashboard than the revenue it costs. Put both numbers in one place before the argument starts.

The generalization beyond geography — any segment that does not recognize your pricing convention (new-to-category buyers, a different buyer persona, a procurement process you do not fit) is a segment where the ask is a wall rather than an offer.

## Apply to your product

- Which of your user segments converts at a rate indistinguishable from zero? What does the paid surface cost you in activation there?
- How early does a new user meet your pricing? Could they reach the core outcome once before you ask?
- If you deleted the paid tier for one segment for a quarter, what number would tell you whether it worked, and do you currently collect it?

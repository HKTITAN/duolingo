# Resemblance Is Not Lineage

## Concept

Two systems that sit next to each other for long enough converge. They swap vocabulary, notation, structure and quirks — and the observable signature of that swapping is identical to the signature of shared origin. So a resemblance you can see tells you almost nothing about how it arose, and the evidence that would distinguish the two explanations is usually the evidence you do not have. The discriminating test is not in the borrowed inventory but in the machinery that generates new material. Borrowed items are a list; generative rules are a system, and systems do not transfer the way items do.

## What Duolingo does

- Persian looks like Arabic at every surface a casual observer checks — the same script, the shared greeting *salām*, and a large borrowed layer of scientific and governmental vocabulary dating to the 7th-century Islamic conquest. It is nevertheless Indo-European, and more closely related to English than to Arabic. Population scale gives a sense of how much contact was available to do the borrowing — Arabic has roughly **310 million** native speakers, Persian **110 million+**, Hebrew about **9 million**.
- Duolingo's discriminating test is word formation, not vocabulary. Arabic and Hebrew build words from **three-consonant roots with vowel patterns inserted** — K-T-B yields *KaTaBa*, *KaaTiB*. Persian and English never do that; they attach affixes — *teach* becomes *teacher*, *āhan* ("iron") becomes *āhangar* ("blacksmith"). The borrowed words moved; the generative rule did not (blog.duolingo.com/are-arabic-hebrew-persian-related (Duolingo blog, 2024-03-19; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The general case has a name. A *sprachbund* is a group of unrelated or distantly related languages that share features from long contact rather than common descent. Duolingo's survey of **22 language families**, out of roughly **7,000 languages** worldwide, names it as the standing objection to the proposed Altaic family grouping Turkic, Mongolic, Japonic and Koreanic, and as the competing explanation for Aymaran and Quechuan overlap and for click consonants in Bantu languages neighbouring Khoe-Kwadi (blog.duolingo.com/language-families-overview (Duolingo blog, 2025-03-04; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The tension worth keeping: the generative test is decisive when it comes back negative and merely suggestive when it comes back positive. Deep machinery can also be borrowed under long enough contact, and the disputes above have run for decades without resolution. The honest output is often "contact is not ruled out", which is less satisfying than a family tree and more accurate.

## The transferable pattern

- **Separate the borrowed inventory from the generating rules.** Names, notation, terminology, file layouts and visible conventions move easily between neighbouring systems. The rules that produce new instances move much less, so that is where the discriminating evidence lives.
- **Ask what else could produce this resemblance** before you conclude common origin. Proximity, a shared upstream dependency, a shared hiring pool, a shared regulator and a shared textbook all manufacture convergence that looks like descent.
- **Count the exceptions, not the matches.** Shared items accumulate from any source; shared irregularities — the arbitrary cases nobody would copy on purpose — are much harder to explain by borrowing.
- **Weight the test by direction.** Different machinery is strong evidence against a shared origin. Identical machinery is consistent with shared origin *and* with deep, sustained contact, so treat it as necessary and not sufficient.
- **Date the resemblance if you can.** A shared feature that appears in both systems at the same moment points at contact; one present in both from the start points at origin. Timestamps, changelogs and version history are the cheapest lineage evidence most teams already have and never look at.
- **Beware the resemblance you introduced.** If one system was built by people who had studied the other, you are looking at borrowing you caused, and calling it convergence flatters everyone involved.
- **Say "not distinguishable on this evidence" when that is the answer.** A confident lineage claim built on surface overlap is the single easiest inference error to make and the hardest to retract, because the surface evidence stays just as visible after you are wrong.

## Apply to your product

- Where have you concluded that two things in your system are related — two data sources, two teams' processes, two products, two user segments — based on how similar they look rather than on how they are generated?
- What is the equivalent of a generative rule in your domain, and can you actually inspect it for both systems, or only their outputs?
- Which competing explanation for the resemblance have you not yet ruled out, and what evidence would rule it out?

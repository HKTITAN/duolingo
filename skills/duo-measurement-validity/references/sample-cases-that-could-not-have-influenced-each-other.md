# Sample Cases That Could Not Have Influenced Each Other

## Concept

Counting agreements is only evidence if the agreeing cases could have disagreed independently. Cases that share an origin, a supplier, a playbook or a borrowing path are not separate observations — they are one observation reported several times, and stacking more of them raises your confidence without raising your evidence. Independence is what converts a count into a claim. The corollary is that the most informative case to check is the one most predisposed to disagree, because agreement there cannot be explained away by contact.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/words-shared-in-all-languages-suggestions (Duolingo blog, 2023-10-17; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Investigating whether any word is genuinely universal, Duolingo states the sampling rule outright — agreement across German, Spanish, Russian and Greek proves little, because those languages are related and in constant contact. Agreement across German, Arabic, Korean and Thai is the finding, because those four have no shared path to copy from.
- It then constructs an adversarial sample on purpose, naming **Chinese, Hebrew and Icelandic** as communities that deliberately coin new words rather than borrow them. That sample does the work — *television*, *telephone* and *radio* all turn out to have unrelated native forms there, so each candidate is knocked out by the cases most likely to knock it out.
- **11 reader-submitted candidates were rejected**, and exactly one additional universal survived — *ok*.
- Exclusion criteria were published rather than hidden. *Amen* and *hallelujah* were ruled out on population grounds, since **more than two-thirds of the world's population is neither Christian nor Jewish**, so their spread traces a shared religious transmission path rather than independent invention.
- The tension is that Duolingo flags one of its own criteria as not a criterion at all. Technology terms were excluded, in the post's words, "for a sort of selfish reason: I think they're not surprising" — an interestingness filter presented alongside methodological ones. Naming it is better practice than most published method sections, and it still means the candidate list was shaped by taste.

## The transferable pattern

- **Before counting agreements, map the paths between your cases.** Same vendor, same consultancy, same conference circuit, same upstream dependency, same founding team — any of these makes agreement expected regardless of whether the claim is true.
- **Discount correlated cases to one.** Ten teams that all copied the same influential write-up are one data point about that write-up. This is the error behind most "everyone does X" claims, and it survives review because the count is genuinely large.
- **Go looking for the population that has reason to disagree.** The holdouts, the different regulatory regime, the competitor's installed base, the team that rejected this approach two years ago. A pattern that survives the adversarial sample is worth more than a hundred agreements inside your own network.
- **Publish your exclusion rules with the result.** A filtered candidate list without its filter is unauditable, and the filter is where a reviewer would have found the problem.
- **Label taste as taste.** If a case was dropped because it was boring rather than because it failed a rule, say so. A criterion that is really a preference will quietly shape every conclusion downstream of it.

## Apply to your product

- The last time you validated something with "we checked with several customers and they all agreed" — how many independent paths were actually in that sample, and how many were the same path counted twice?
- Who is the population most likely to reject your claim, and what would it cost to put the question to them specifically this month?
- Which cases did you exclude from your last analysis, is the exclusion rule written down, and would you be comfortable publishing it alongside the result?

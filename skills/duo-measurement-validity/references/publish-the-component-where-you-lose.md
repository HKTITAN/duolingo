# Publish the Component Where You Lose

## Concept

An aggregate can be carried by one strong component while a weak one silently caps the ceiling. Only decomposition tells you where to invest — and the same decomposition is the cheapest credibility you will ever buy. Readers discount uniformly positive results, because uniformly positive results are what marketing produces. One admitted weak spot changes the genre of the whole report from claim to measurement. The cost is real and should be priced in advance, because the weak number is the one that gets quoted.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-well-does-duolingo-teach-speaking-skills (Duolingo blog, 2021-06-01; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo broke its speaking result into four sub-scores rather than reporting one figure, and published the full spread rather than the headline.
- Spanish pronunciation was the **highest sub-score of either language**, with roughly **90% of learners reaching B1 or higher** and the group average nearly hitting B1.
- French pronunciation was the **single lowest sub-score in the study**, with only about **22% reaching B1 or higher** — and Duolingo stated plainly that it was dragging the overall French result down.
- Rather than leaving it as an admission, the post named it as work to do and pointed at teaching tools being built from anonymised speech data. The weak number arrived attached to a plan.
- The tension to price in: a published low number is quotable out of context, and "app is worst at pronunciation" is a more forwardable sentence than the 90% figure next to it. Duolingo absorbed that in exchange for the rest of the report reading as evidence. If you are not prepared to be quoted on your worst dimension, you are not ready to publish any of them.

## The transferable pattern

- **Decompose before you interpret.** A flat aggregate cannot tell you whether you have a broad mediocrity problem or one component pinning the ceiling. Those need opposite investments, and the composite is identical in both cases.
- **Lead with the spread, not the average.** Report the best and worst component in the same breath. The distance between them is the actual finding.
- **Ship the weak number with its remediation.** A disclosed weakness with no plan reads as a confession. The same weakness with a named next step reads as a roadmap, and costs you nothing extra to write.
- **Publish the comparison the weak number invites, before someone else does.** If a competitor beats you on that component, saying so costs less than being caught omitting it, and it makes your other comparisons believable.
- **Expect the weak number to travel further than the strong one.** Decide in advance whether the credibility is worth that trade. For an audience that has to trust you without being able to verify you — buyers, regulators, press, an internal board — it almost always is. For a purely promotional surface it may not be, and the honest move is then to publish nothing rather than publish only the wins.

## Apply to your product

- What is the single worst component inside the metric you quote publicly, and who in your company currently knows that number?
- If you published it next week, what is the worst sentence someone could write about you — and is there a plan you could publish alongside it that makes that sentence incomplete?
- Are any of your reported figures uniformly positive across every dimension? If so, either the breakdown is too coarse to be informative, or you are only reporting the dimensions you win on.

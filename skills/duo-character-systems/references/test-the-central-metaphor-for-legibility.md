# Test the Central Metaphor for Legibility

## Concept

Every product has one or two load-bearing metaphors — the visual shorthand that explains the core mechanic without words. Those metaphors feel universal to the team that chose them because the team shares a culture. They are not universal; they are learned. When one fails to translate, the cost lands on exactly the users who can least afford it, the newest ones, and it lands silently. Nobody files a ticket saying they did not understand an icon. They just engage a little less, and that signal never reaches you through normal feedback channels.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/streak-milestone-design-animation (Duolingo blog, 2022-01-21; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Research and focus groups showed that **some new learners did not understand the streak at all**. The team's hypothesis was that the carrier metaphor — "keeping the flame alive," being "on fire" — is not a shared analogy globally, even though it is obvious in the cultures the design team came from.
- The problem surfaced only because Duolingo went looking for it. It had not arrived as complaints.
- The fix kept the concept and changed the carrier. They moved to a **phoenix**, chosen on the reasoning that **a bird in profile recurs across flags and coats of arms worldwide** — so the fire metaphor is preserved inside a form with much broader reach.
- Tension: the legible direction was not the fun direction. Earlier sketch directions — Duo roasting a s'more, carrying a candle, a lab beaker — were rejected as not scalable and still not universal. The team had to give up concepts that tested better internally in exchange for ones that read more widely.

## The transferable pattern

Separate the **concept** from its **carrier**. The concept is what you need the user to understand: continuity, progress, danger, ownership, urgency. The carrier is the specific image you chose to convey it. Concepts travel; carriers frequently do not, and you can almost always swap the carrier without touching the mechanic.

How to find the failures, given that they never arrive as bug reports:

1. **Go look on purpose.** Put the icon or illustration in front of new users from outside your home market with no label and no context, and ask what they think it means. Comprehension failures in your newest cohort are invisible in aggregate engagement data.
2. **Prefer carriers with wide independent reach.** A form that already recurs across many unrelated traditions has a high prior of being read correctly, which is a better selection criterion than novelty.
3. **Expect the internal favourite to lose.** Charm and legibility are different axes, and the version that delights the team is frequently the version that depends most on shared context. Decide in advance which axis wins.
4. **Check the metaphor again when you enter a market,** not once at launch. The carrier that works in three markets may be the reason adoption stalls in the fourth.

## Apply to your product

- Write down the one or two metaphors your product depends on and name the concept each one is carrying. Could the concept survive a different carrier?
- Have you ever shown your core icons, with labels removed, to someone outside your home market? What did they say they meant?
- Which visual in your product would you be most embarrassed to learn is misread? That is the one worth putting in front of strangers this month.

# Put Expensive Delight Where the Traffic Is

## Concept

The cost of building a beautiful thing is fixed. Its return scales with how many times it is seen. That asymmetry has an obvious consequence that teams routinely ignore: the most expensive asset in the product belongs on the surface with the most impressions, not behind the rare trigger that felt like the most special place to put it. The same arithmetic governs whether a second surface deserves to exist at all, and it explains why motion is worth more on a decision screen than anywhere else.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/building-character (Duolingo blog, 2020-11-10; accessed 2026-09-22)

- After **18 months** of character design, Duolingo deliberately placed the cast "in places where learners spent most of their time" rather than in a showcase surface.
- Concretely: the characters were designed into the **majority of exercises** as the speakers of the sentence being translated, given a **unique per-character animation on every correct answer**, and used in mid-lesson interstitial animations rewarding streaks of correct answers. The 18-month spend is amortized across every single exercise.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/shape-language-duolingos-art-style (Duolingo blog, 2020-07-02; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo animated its skill icons specifically to bring movement to a high-return screen, and found the icons became attention-grabbers in their own right — learners watch the full animation where they merely glance at a static illustration. The extra dwell is time the surrounding prompt is being read.
- The art team also rotates lines and angles inside artwork to steer the eye toward the UI or text that matters, which is the same job done statically.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/widget-feature (Duolingo blog, 2023-08-29; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Learners told Duolingo the home-screen widget was about as effective as push notifications, which made the team nervous that it was just another notification layer.
- The response was to make it trade on something a notification physically cannot offer — persistent space. They reskinned it with illustrations showing the mascot's mood shifting by time of day and by whether you had done your lesson, rolling out roughly **25 new illustrations** in the second wave.
- Tension: high traffic is also high repetition. An asset in the core loop is seen thousands of times by the same person and wears out, which is why the widget set varies by time and state instead of repeating one image, and why per-character variants exist rather than one shared celebration.

## The transferable pattern

Do the multiplication before you commission the work. Asset cost is a one-time number; impressions per month is the multiplier. A gorgeous thing behind a trigger that fires for two percent of users once a quarter is a gorgeous thing nobody sees, and it competes for the same budget as the thing everyone sees every day.

Three corollaries:

- **Put the flagship spend in the repeated loop**, then design variation into it, because the same surface that gives you the impressions gives you the wear.
- **Use motion where a decision happens.** The eye prioritizes movement and will wait out a cycle, where it parses a static image in a fraction of a second and moves on. That extra dwell is the window in which the call to action gets read. Elsewhere, motion is mostly a tax.
- **Make a second surface earn its existence structurally.** Channels compete for one attention budget, so a new one that merely repeats what an existing one delivers has negative marginal value — it costs installation, space and tolerance. It has to trade on something the old channel cannot do, usually persistence or real estate, not on frequency.

## Apply to your product

- Rank your surfaces by monthly impressions, then rank them by how much design effort they have absorbed. Where do the two lists disagree most?
- On your highest-intent screen, what is currently static that could hold attention for one extra second, and what would the user read during that second?
- If you are about to add a new channel or surface, name the thing it can do that your existing one physically cannot. If the answer is "the same, but more often," you are building an uninstall.

# Humor Is Functional, Not Decorative

## Concept

In any product where the user is expected to fail repeatedly — practice, training, diagnostics, anything with a skill curve — the barrier to trying again is rarely difficulty. It is the cost of looking foolish. A visual and verbal register that is obviously not serious changes that calculation directly: it signals that mistakes here do not count against you, which lowers the threshold for another attempt. Delight in this context is not garnish. It is buying a specific behavior — repeated voluntary exposure to failure — that a neutral, competent interface does not get.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/shape-language-duolingos-art-style (Duolingo blog, 2020-07-02; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo's artists use **exaggeration "almost to the point of caricature"**, combined with rhythm — a strategic variety of shapes and sizes — rather than drawing subjects straight.
- They deliberately look for the quirky story hidden inside a constrained prompt instead of illustrating it literally. Asked to illustrate the sentence "I like this shirt," the team built a much odder story around it.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-silly-sentences-can-help-you-learn (Duolingo blog, 2021-03-03; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The sequence is explicit and non-negotiable. Writers **plan the learning objective and the required grammar first**, and only then are "encouraged to develop humorous, interesting content."
- The example given, "Tu habites avec un cheval ?", is memorable nonsense that simultaneously carries question formation, gender and verb ending. The absurdity is a retrieval anchor for the abstract pattern hidden inside it — odd content is encoded more strongly.
- Tension: the ordering is easy to invert and inverting it is fatal. Lead with the joke and the joke becomes the content, with the objective bent to fit the gag. Humor also does not travel evenly, and a register that reads as warm in one market can read as unserious or juvenile in another, which is a real cost for products where credibility is part of the value.

## The transferable pattern

Treat playfulness as a lever on a specific behavior, then check whether that behavior is one you need:

- **If your users must be wrong repeatedly to get good**, an unserious register is functional and you should budget for it like any other retention feature. The thing you are lowering is not difficulty but the perceived stakes of an attempt.
- **If your users are being trusted with something consequential** — money, health, someone else's data — the same register cuts the other way, because the signal "mistakes here do not count" is false and the user knows it.

When you do use it, keep the order strict. Specify the objective and the required content first, then let writers and illustrators find the absurdity inside those constraints. That ordering is the only reliable guard against the humor becoming the product. A useful working method is to refuse the literal reading of a brief and look for the odd story implied by it — the literal illustration is forgettable precisely because it adds nothing the text did not already say.

## Apply to your product

- Does your product require users to be wrong on the way to being good? If yes, what in your current interface tells them that is expected rather than embarrassing?
- Where does your product currently illustrate things literally, restating in a picture what the text already says? Those are free slots for something memorable.
- Who on your team writes the objective, and who writes the joke — and which one goes first in your actual process today?

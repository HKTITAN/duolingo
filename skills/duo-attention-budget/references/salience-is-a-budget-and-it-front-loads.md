# Salience Is a Budget, and It Front-Loads

## Concept

Attention does not distribute; it amplifies. Whatever wins gets processed across more of the brain and is therefore more likely to be remembered — and everything else is actively suppressed. So if a decoration, an animation, or a novel control is more salient than the concept you are teaching, the concept is not encoded at all. Salience is a budget you spend, not a free property you add.

The second half is that the budget is unevenly distributed across a sequence. Processing capacity is spent as the sequence unfolds, and the reader commits to an interpretation early, then checks later material against that commitment rather than reading it fresh. Anything late only registers if it contradicts loudly.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-to-make-your-brain-pay-attention (Duolingo blog, 2023-08-08; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Lessons focus on **just a couple of related topics at a time**, chosen so learners notice the specific details that matter — new vocabulary, improper fractions — rather than the equivalent of the paintings on the coffee-shop wall. The paintings are the post's own image for a competing signal that wins by being more interesting than the lesson.
- The stated mechanism: attention works by amplifying one signal and suppressing the rest, and what gets attended gets processed more deeply.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/common-mistakes-language-learners (Duolingo blog, 2023-08-22; accessed 2026-09-22)

- The **first-noun strategy**: learners assume the first noun is the subject, so they misread passives like "The cat was chased by the dog." The parser commits early and the later contradicting information loses.
- A general primacy effect, in Duolingo's words: our brains "sort of run out of steam later in a sentence" — while acknowledging that important grammatical information is often tucked away at the end anyway. That is the honest version: the constraint is real *and* you cannot always honour it, because meaning is sometimes genuinely carried at the end.

## The transferable pattern

- **Audit salience, not just presence.** The question is never "is it on the screen" but "what is the loudest thing on this screen, and is it the thing I want processed?" A count of elements tells you nothing; a squint test tells you everything.
- **Decoration competes.** An illustration, a badge, a motion flourish, or a novel control is not free — it is spending from the same budget as your content. Every added element demands a justification against the thing it will suppress. What that flourish should look like is a separate craft question; here the only claim is that it costs.
- **Put what must be noticed first.** Early positions in a sentence, a form, a list, a flow, or an email get the deepest processing. Later positions get checked against a commitment already made.
- **Never let a terminal element carry the load.** A caveat at the bottom, a state change in the last field, a negation at the end of a sentence — these are structurally likely to be missed. If something at the end must be noticed, it has to contradict loudly or move.
- **Expect early commitment and design for correction.** Users lock in an interpretation fast and then defend it. Where a late element flips the meaning, surface the flip rather than assuming it will be read.

## Apply to your product

- Squint at your most important screen. What is loudest? If it is not the thing the screen exists for, what is it competing with?
- Where does meaning in your product depend on something at the end — the last field of a form, the final clause of a confirmation, a footnote under a chart?
- What interpretation do users commit to in the first two seconds of your key flow, and what do you do when the rest of the flow contradicts it?

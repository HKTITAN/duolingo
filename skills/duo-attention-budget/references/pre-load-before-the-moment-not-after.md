# Pre-Load Before the Moment, Not After

## Concept

Recognition has to keep up with a live situation. If it arrives after the moment has moved on, it is worthless — the user has already guessed, already clicked, already been left behind. Priming fixes this by moving the handful of relevant items into short-term memory *before* the moment and narrowing the search space at the instant of comprehension.

Three levers do this work, all cheap: a tiny pre-load of the specific items about to appear, an explicit script for the sequence about to unfold, and a single line naming the goal — because attention filters for whatever it has been told matters, and without a stated criterion the filter defaults to whatever is loudest.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/context-clues-while-traveling (Duolingo blog, 2026-04-07; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo names the technique — **semantic priming** — and gives an operational recipe: before walking into a setting, think of **five words** you are likely to hear.
  - Cafe: coffee, milk, sugar, eat here, take away.
  - Pharmacy: pain, fever, prescription, dose, sleep.
- **Five, not fifty.** The size is the point: a set small enough to actually hold is what makes recognition fast enough to track live speech.
- It also teaches the **script**: most daily interactions follow a fixed sequence — in a shop, greeting, then price, then thank you — and knowing your position in the script tells you what the words in that slot must mean. Same for setting-based inference: at a train station, announcements are almost certainly arrivals or delays, so the setting carries meaning the content does not have to.

Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-to-make-your-brain-pay-attention (Duolingo blog, 2023-08-08; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo's learning scientist prescribes identifying your end goal and what you need to do to get there **before** starting a lesson — named as metacognition, and grouped with the reason cramming fails for long-term retention. The mechanism: instructions and context direct attention, so naming the end state first gives the filter its criterion.
- The limit worth stating: priming only works if you can predict the handful of items. Where the space is genuinely open, a pre-load of the wrong five is worse than none, because it biases the filter toward things that never appear.

## The transferable pattern

- **Pre-load a handful, not a catalogue.** Three to five items the user is about to meet, delivered at the threshold of the moment. Reference documentation is for after the fact; a pre-load is for during.
- **Put it at the threshold.** Same screen, immediately before. A pre-load one screen earlier has already decayed; a reference one click away is never opened mid-task.
- **Give them the script.** For any recurring sequence, tell the user what happens first, second, third. Position in a known sequence constrains an unparseable element to the small set of things that could fill that slot, so partial understanding becomes sufficient instead of merely frustrating.
- **State the outcome before the task.** One line naming what they are about to achieve changes what they notice throughout. Without it, their filter has no criterion and defaults to whatever is most salient.
- **Only prime what you can predict.** If the next moment is genuinely open-ended, a confident wrong pre-load is worse than silence.

## Apply to your product

- Name the five things a user must recognise in your hardest moment. Where could those five appear right before that moment instead of in a help article?
- Which of your recurring flows have an implicit script that you have never written down for the user?
- Does the top of your key screens state the outcome the user is working toward, or does it start with controls?

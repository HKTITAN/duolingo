# Anchor To An External Published Standard

## Concept

A level number you invented has meaning only inside your product. Nobody outside can accept it, nobody can dispute it, and the user cannot spend it. Mapping your progression onto a standard that institutions already use converts in-app progress into something exportable — which, for most users, is the thing they were actually buying. It also disciplines you: once each level claims a public meaning, you have to be honest about what that level actually confers. The catch is that a standard is a skeleton, not a curriculum. It tells you what a level should amount to, never how to get someone there.

## What Duolingo does

- Every new course is built against **CEFR** and US national standards rather than a Duolingo-invented difficulty scale. The framing is explicit: anchoring this way is what lets a learner describe their progress to people anywhere in the world.
- CEFR's **six levels** are A1 and A2 (Basic), B1 and B2 (Independent), C1 and C2 (Proficient).
- Tension: the standard is a skeleton. CEFR says a learner at a given level should be able to ask about where someone lives and gives no more detail than that, so Duolingo still had to do the entire sequencing research itself. Anchoring removed the legibility problem, not the design work.
- Source: blog.duolingo.com/the-nuts-and-bolts-of-course-creation-at-duolingo (Duolingo blog, 2020-06-11; accessed 2026-09-22)
- Duolingo then expresses proficiency in those terms consistently and publishes per-level can-do statements — from "you can introduce yourself and say what you do" at A1 through adapting smoothly to conversations on unfamiliar topics in noisy environments at C2 — and pushes learners to use the standard to set concrete short-term goals rather than open-ended ones.
- Source: blog.duolingo.com/can-you-learn-a-language-in-6-months (Duolingo blog, 2026-08-18; accessed 2026-09-22)

## The transferable pattern

- **Find the scale your users' counterparties already read.** The test is not whether the standard is elegant; it is whether an employer, a regulator, a buyer or a certifying body already knows what its levels mean without you explaining.
- **Map onto it rather than beside it.** A private scale with a published conversion table is still a private scale — people have to trust your table. Stating the level directly removes that dependency.
- **Publish a can-do statement per level.** Say what a person at each level can actually do, in observable terms. That is what makes the level checkable by someone who was not there, and it stops levels from quietly inflating.
- **Expect the standard to underspecify.** It defines the destination and almost never the route, so all the sequencing, pacing and content research stays yours. Anchoring buys legibility, not a curriculum.
- **Keep the mapping auditable.** Write down which of your levels claims which external level and on what evidence, and date it. Anchoring is a claim an outsider can check, so the moment your content moves without the mapping being revisited, you are making a claim you can no longer defend.
- **Know when it constrains you.** An external standard is a commitment: you now owe outsiders the meaning they read into it, which limits how freely you can re-cut your levels later. That is the price of the progress being worth something.

## Apply to your product

- What scale, certification, rubric or rating do your users' employers, customers or regulators already understand — and what is your current level system worth outside your app compared with it?
- If you published a can-do statement for each of your levels, which one would you be uncomfortable writing down? That is the level that does not mean anything yet.
- Where does the external standard stop specifying, and who on your team owns the sequencing work it leaves open?

# Interview the Real Work

## Concept

Most interview loops measure a proxy: a greenfield algorithm puzzle, a polished portfolio walkthrough, a hypothetical case. Each of those measures something real, but rarely the thing the job contains. The daily failure mode of an engineer is misreading a codebase someone else wrote; the daily failure mode of a designer is reasoning under an unfamiliar constraint with incomplete information. Neither surfaces in a format the candidate has rehearsed and controls.

The rule: for each core skill of the role, name the medium in which it is actually exercised, then build one stage that puts the candidate in that medium unrehearsed.

## What Duolingo does

- **Engineering loop: 75-minute pair programming session in a codebase "similar to what we use in production," plus a separate code review session** where the candidate critiques a set of changes another engineer made. Both stages are explicitly about working with code the candidate did not write. Every other session runs 60 minutes. Source: blog.duolingo.com/interviewing-with-duolingos-engineering-team (Duolingo blog, 2024-08-20; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **Product design onsite: five sessions, one at 45 minutes and four at 30 (one with 15 minutes of Q&A).** The case-study presentation must be a purpose-built narrative, explicitly not a click-through of the portfolio. The 45-minute "design sense" session runs live in Figma and is described as equal parts critique and thinking on the fly. Source: blog.duolingo.com/product-design-interviews (Duolingo blog, 2023-05-09; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **PM take-home is a compressed version of the job**: research real users, pick a problem, propose something that moves a key metric, defend it to a committee. One hire spent several days on app reviews and learner conversations before presenting — and decided during the exercise that he wanted the role. Source: blog.duolingo.com/principal-product-manager-interview (Duolingo blog, 2022-12-16; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **The individual-contributor exercise stays in the manager loop.** A senior engineering director, initially skeptical of being asked to pair-program, called it "one of the smartest differentiators in our process" — the point was not the solution but how he explained his thinking and took feedback. Source: blog.duolingo.com/engineering-leadership-first-year-duolingo (Duolingo blog, 2025-10-06; accessed 2026-09-22)

## The transferable pattern

1. **Write down the three things the role fails at.** Not the three things it does — the three ways a competent-looking hire goes wrong in month four. Those are your stages.
2. **At least one stage must use artifacts the candidate did not author.** Reading, extending and critiquing someone else's work is most of most jobs and none of most interviews.
3. **Keep the leadership loop hands-on.** If a manager will set the quality bar for a craft, the loop should observe them reasoning inside that craft, not only describing it.

Tensions worth naming. A realistic simulation costs more than a puzzle — days of candidate time for a take-home, senior hours for a live critique — and it screens in both directions, which is a feature only if you can afford to lose people who discover the job is not for them. A hard loop also sends a signal: one hire read the difficulty of the process as evidence "the team cared about keeping the bar high," but the same difficulty deters candidates who are weighing several offers. Source: blog.duolingo.com/why-i-chose-duolingo-fabio-lessa (Duolingo blog, 2020-10-26; accessed 2026-09-22)

## Apply to your product

- Which stage of your current loop would a strong candidate pass while still failing at the job? What is it actually measuring?
- Does any stage put the candidate in front of work they did not produce — a review, a critique, an inherited system?
- If you hire managers of a craft, does your loop ever watch them do the craft, or only talk about it?

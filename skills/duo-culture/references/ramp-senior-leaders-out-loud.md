# Ramp Senior Leaders Out Loud

## Concept

A senior hire with no stated ramp assumes the clock is short. Assuming the clock is short, they optimize for visible action over correct action — and the expensive failure mode of a new leader is not inaction, it is a reorganization or a strategy shift made before they had the context to make it. Naming a number converts anxious activity into deliberate listening.

The second half of the rule is structural: put the new leader briefly inside each layer they will eventually oversee. Decisions made from the top impose costs that are invisible from the top — coordination load, review burden, context-switching — and someone who has personally carried a layer feels those as concrete rather than abstract.

## What Duolingo does

- **Senior engineering leaders onboard over 3 to 6+ months**, framed by the "take the long view" operating principle. A senior product leader told an incoming director: "At most companies, leaders need to show impact immediately because the average tenure is short. At Duolingo, we expect leaders to be here for a long time, so 3–6 months of onboarding is not a big deal." Source: blog.duolingo.com/engineering-leadership-first-year-duolingo (Duolingo blog, 2025-10-06; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **The ramp goes up through the layers, not straight to the top.** Anand Mariappan started as an engineer on the team he would later lead, became team lead of about 10 people after one month, area lead of about 70 after four months, then pillar lead of about 150.
- **The payoff showed up in a later reorganization**, where he made choices "that considered the overhead area leads would face, something I wouldn't have appreciated without walking that path myself."
- **A related calibration is taught explicitly**: leaders must act on less information than they would like, and the sorting question is reversibility, not certainty. Completely irreversible workplace decisions are rare; the required skill is the humility to admit a failure and reverse course. Presented at Duolingo's third annual Engineering Summit, June 2026. Source: blog.duolingo.com/engineering-leadership-lessons (Duolingo blog, 2026-09-16; accessed 2026-09-22)

## The transferable pattern

1. **State the ramp as a number, in the offer conversation.** "We expect the first quarter to be listening" is an instruction; silence is an instruction too, and a worse one.
2. **Sequence the ramp through the layers they will later restructure.** A month carrying a small team's overhead buys judgment that no briefing document transfers.
3. **Teach the reversibility sort early.** The cost of a delayed decision is paid continuously by everyone blocked behind it; the cost of a wrong-but-reversible decision is paid once and recovered. Route deliberation to the genuinely one-way choices and clear the rest fast.

Tensions worth naming. A long ramp only pencils out if tenure is genuinely long — it is an investment repaid over years, and an organization with high churn is buying an asset it will not hold. Saying the number also does not remove the pressure: the same director found patience "the hardest adjustment," worried about whether he was having impact quickly enough, and had to drive his own onboarding because the company "was still evolving its approach for senior leaders." And the model is explicitly not for everyone — "the bar is high."

## Apply to your product

- What did the last senior person you hired believe about how quickly they needed to show a result? Did anyone tell them, or did they infer it?
- Before someone redesigns a layer of your organization, have they ever worked inside it?
- Which decision is currently stalled in your team waiting for more data — and is it actually reversible?

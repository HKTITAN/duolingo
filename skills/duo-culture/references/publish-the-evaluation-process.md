# Publish the Evaluation Process

## Concept

An unpublished interview format is a hidden variable. Two candidates of identical ability score differently because one has a network that has seen the format and one does not — so part of your signal is a measure of prior access, dressed up as a measure of ability. Publishing the format removes that variable without lowering the bar: knowing that a session is 75 minutes of pair programming does not make anyone better at pair programming.

The same logic applies to instructions candidates are silently penalized for missing. If interviewers want a specific behaviour, the cheap fix is to say so in advance rather than to score people on having guessed.

## What Duolingo does

- **The full engineering interview plan is public**: every interview type, the minutes allotted to each (75 for pair programming, 60 for everything else), the languages permitted, and what each session is looking for. Source: blog.duolingo.com/interviewing-with-duolingos-engineering-team (Duolingo blog, 2024-08-20; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **In-session guidance is published too, not implied**: think out loud, start with the simplest approach that works, ask clarifying questions rather than enlarging the problem, and expect interviewers to give hints. Hints being an announced part of the format changes what taking one means.
- **Attribution is stated as an explicit instruction**: "make your role clear" — say "I did this," not "we did this." Collective phrasing is polite and habitual, so candidates default to it without intending to mislead and interviewers are left guessing at contribution. Publishing the expectation removes the guessing without penalizing the well-mannered. Source: blog.duolingo.com/product-design-interviews (Duolingo blog, 2023-05-09; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **The design onsite publishes its structure the same way**: five sessions, what each one probes (case-study narrative, live critique, collaboration with a partner function, process and critical thinking, a manager conversation), and how long each runs.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Publish the shape, keep the content fresh.** Formats, durations, tools, what each stage looks for, and how to prepare are all safe to disclose. The specific problem is not — rotate it.
2. **Anything you penalize silently, state loudly.** "Ambiguous attribution," "did not ask clarifying questions," "over-scoped" are only fair scoring criteria once they are announced criteria.
3. **Treat the published document as a commitment.** Once it is public, drift between the document and the real loop is visible to every candidate, which is precisely the pressure that keeps the loop consistent between interviewers.

Tensions worth naming. Publishing trades surprise for fairness, and surprise has some value — a rehearsed answer to a known prompt tells you less than a first reaction. The mitigation is to publish the container and vary the contents, but this is a real cost, not a free win. A public process also invites comparison and complaint: every rejected candidate can now check your behaviour against your own document, which is the point and also the price.

## Apply to your product

- Could a candidate with no connection to anyone at your company learn what your interview stages are and how long they run?
- List three things your interviewers score that have never been said to a candidate. Which of those are guessable, and which are insider knowledge?
- If two interviewers ran the same stage this month, would a candidate have experienced the same format?

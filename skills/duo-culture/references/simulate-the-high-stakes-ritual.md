# Simulate the High-Stakes Ritual

## Concept

Shadowing an expert does not transfer judgment, because it keeps the newcomer in observation mode. The skill being taught is decision-making under evaluation, and that skill is only exercised by being evaluated. Apprenticeship also varies wildly by mentor, so two people hired the same week get different curricula and nobody can say why one ramped faster.

The alternative: find the ritual where your organization's judgment is actually tested — the review, the design crit, the incident call, the pricing committee — and build a replica with the same format, the same audience, and the stakes removed.

## What Duolingo does

- **Product Bootcamp is three days in which a new PM takes an idea through the full product lifecycle and ends by presenting a mock Product Review to senior leaders including the CEO** — evaluated like the real thing, with nothing riding on it. Teams of three (PM, product designer, user researcher) work a well-scoped real problem, such as improving onboarding for the Math course. First run March 2023; three held as of March 2025. Source: blog.duolingo.com/product-bootcamp (Duolingo blog, 2025-03-14; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **It was built to replace something they named as broken**: shadowing experienced PMs was "highly variable across individuals" and let new hires stay in the training-wheels phase too long.
- **V1 spent roughly 1.5 of 3 days on instruction; V2 spends all 3 on the project.** Feedback said the program felt rushed and impersonal, and that the five skill sessions were not relevant to everyone. Those sessions became "Product School" — on-demand training completable any time between start date and bootcamp — which was then opened to the whole company.
- **Success measures were agreed with leadership before launch**, using the same internal framework the company uses for products ([[green-machine]]): leadership perception of new-PM onboarding, observed directly by attending the mock reviews, and new-PM perception of learning and development via the product org engagement score, which rose after the program started.
- **The format was cloned across functions.** Engineering built "Hootcamp," modeled on Product Bootcamp; design began building its own.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Pick the ritual, not the curriculum.** Ask where in your organization someone's judgment is publicly tested. Rebuild that event, keep the real evaluators in the room, remove the consequences.
2. **Move concepts to self-paced material and spend live time only on what must be live.** Synchronous hours are the scarce resource, and background knowledge is exactly where individual need varies most — a shared lecture over-serves half the room and under-serves the other half.
3. **Build the program like a product**: a named user need, success measures agreed before launch, a deliberately rough first version, and a feedback loop. A program with no defined metric cannot be improved or defended, and is cancelled the first quarter things get tight.
4. **Once one function proves the format, let the others clone it.** The expensive part was designing the format — problem scoping, evaluation ritual, feedback loop — not the domain content.

Tensions worth naming. Both of Duolingo's headline metrics are perception-based, and leadership "anecdotally felt" the improvement carried through to teams; no time-to-productivity number is published. This is a program judged on impressions by the people who authorized it, which is the weakest evidence class available. It also consumes senior time on a recurring cadence, which is the cost most organizations quietly refuse to pay.

## Apply to your product

- What is the highest-stakes recurring ritual in your organization, and how does a new hire first experience it — as a participant or as an observer?
- Which parts of your onboarding are being taught live that could be absorbed alone, at each person's own pace, before day one?
- If someone asked you to defend your onboarding program's existence next quarter, what measure would you show them?

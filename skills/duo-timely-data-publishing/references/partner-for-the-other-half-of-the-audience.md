# Partner for the Other Half of the Audience

## Concept

Coverage is won by relevance to a specific readership, and a single aggregate number is relevant to nobody in particular. The same dataset, cut by market, produces one distinct headline per market at no extra research cost — the fieldwork was already paid for, and the cuts are a query. The second multiplier is the co-author. A partner who holds the half of the audience you do not reach doubles the distribution of every one of those cuts, and lends a kind of credibility a single interested vendor cannot manufacture, because two parties with different commercial interests agreeing on a number reads as a finding rather than a pitch.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/state-of-jargon-report (Duolingo blog, 2023-06-13; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo and **LinkedIn** jointly surveyed **over 8,000 working professionals across 8 countries** — India, Vietnam, Colombia, Brazil, the U.K., Japan, Australia and the U.S. — on workplace jargon.
- The deliverable is not one ranking. It is a **top-5 most-confusing-jargon list per country**, so every local outlet has a local headline from the same fieldwork.
- Commentary comes from **both sides** — a Duolingo linguistics expert and a LinkedIn career expert — which is what makes the piece readable as research rather than as either company's marketing.
- Headline figures travel on their own: **58%** say coworkers overuse jargon, **nearly half** would eliminate it entirely, **60%** figure out jargon on their own, and **about 25%** admit using it without realizing.

A second variant of the same move: partnering with survey firms rather than an audience holder, when the thing you need is data your logs structurally cannot contain. For the 2022 language report Duolingo worked with **YouGov, Momentive/SurveyMonkey and Surveroid** on travel intent, and published per-market cuts again — **55% of Latin Americans** more likely to travel internationally in 2023 versus **40% in India, 28% in the U.S., 20% in Germany, 15% in Japan**; **85% of U.S. respondents** agreed the right preparation would make travel more enjoyable; **68% in Latin America** planning a trip somewhere they do not know the language; **32% of Germans** saw no need to prepare versus **6% in Latin America** (blog.duolingo.com/2022-duolingo-language-report (Duolingo blog, 2022-12-06; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**The cost.** Two brands means two approval chains, a shared narrative neither side fully controls, and a partner whose commercial needs may pull the framing. The credibility comes precisely from that loss of control — a report you could have written alone would not have been worth co-signing.

## The transferable pattern

- **Cut the same dataset by the smallest unit that has its own press.** Country, region, industry, company size, role. Each cut is a separate story from one piece of fieldwork.
- **Pick the partner for the audience you are missing**, not the one with the biggest logo. The test is whether their readers are people you cannot currently reach.
- **Put both experts in the piece.** Two named voices from two organizations is what separates a finding from an announcement.
- **Buy the data when your logs cannot hold it.** Behavioural records are strictly retrospective and cannot distinguish "did not want to" from "could not yet." Stated intent is purchasable, and it is what lets a report make a forward claim.
- **Budget for the friction.** Joint sign-off is slower than solo publishing, and that delay is the price of the credibility.

## Apply to your product

- What is the smallest slice of your dataset that still has a publication covering it, and how many such slices do you already have the sample for?
- Who holds the half of your audience you never reach, and what would they get from co-signing your number?
- What claim would you like to make that your logs can never support — and is that claim worth the price of a commissioned survey?

# Baseline and Hedge the Spike

## Concept

When something outside your product moves your numbers, you are holding a fact nobody else has. It is proprietary, timely and quantitative — the three properties that make a fact repeatable by a journalist. But a spike on its own proves nothing, because seasonality, a marketing push, a platform feature or a pricing change all produce the same shape. The two cheap additions that turn the observation into a claim are the identical calendar window from the prior year, and a sentence admitting you are not certain of the cause. Both make the piece *more* credible, not less, because they pre-empt the first objection a sceptical reader would raise.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/squid-game-could-inspire-a-new-wave-of-korean-language-learners (Duolingo blog, 2021-10-04; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Published within weeks of the Netflix premiere, reporting roughly **40% growth in new US learners studying Korean** in the two weeks after the premiere versus the week before.
- Ran the **same calendar window from 2020 alongside it**, showing no comparable spike. That single contrast rules out "this happens every autumn," which is the obvious alternative explanation.
- Hedged in the text — the post says outright it is **"not 100% sure"** the show caused the jump. The hedge is what keeps a correlational claim repeatable instead of overclaimed and later corrected.
- Added user tweets as qualitative texture, so the number has faces attached to it.

A later post applies the same discipline at larger scale: Russian fell out of the global top 10 to **#11** after the February 2022 invasion of Ukraine, while Portuguese rose to **#8** on post-pandemic travel demand and digital nomad visas. It reports **1.3 million new Ukrainian learners** in the weeks after the invasion, with **Germany and Poland over 1600% growth**, over a stated data window of **Oct 1 2022 – Apr 30 2023**, and cites outside reporting for each causal reading (blog.duolingo.com/2023-top-10-languages (Duolingo blog, 2023-05-09; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**Tension.** The Russian decline measures reputation, not usefulness. Russian still has **282M speakers** and had been top-10 since late 2015; what changed was its association with the aggressor. So the signal is a sentiment reading dressed as a demand reading, and a piece that does not say so is inviting a correction.

## The transferable pattern

Ship four things together, or do not ship:

1. **The number only you can see.** An aggregate movement in your own telemetry, stated as a percentage change with both endpoints named.
2. **The same window, one period earlier.** Same length, same calendar position. If the prior period shows the same shape, you do not have a story — you have a season.
3. **An explicit hedge.** Say which direction of causation you can and cannot support. A hedge costs one sentence and buys you the right to be quoted without a caveat someone else writes for you.
4. **The named external cause, with an outside citation.** A ranking change alone is trivia. The interpretation is the product.

Then say what the movement actually measures. A shift in who signs up is usually a reading of sentiment, attention or access — rarely a reading of underlying need. Conflating the two is how a good finding becomes an embarrassing one.

## Apply to your product

- Which external events reliably move a number in your telemetry, and how fast could you pull the same window from last year?
- What is the most plausible boring explanation for your next spike, and can you rule it out in one chart?
- When your data moves, are you measuring what people need, or how they currently feel about something?

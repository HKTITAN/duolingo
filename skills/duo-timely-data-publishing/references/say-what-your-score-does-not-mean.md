# Say What Your Score Does Not Mean

## Concept

Any number attached to a named person or organization will be read as a verdict on the quality that number vaguely resembles, no matter what it actually measures. A readability score becomes a ranking of intelligence. A response-time score becomes a ranking of customer care. A complexity index becomes a ranking of competence. Readers do this instantly and headline writers do it deliberately, so the correction has to arrive before the misreading does — in the body of the piece, on first publication. Afterwards it is not a paragraph, it is a week of correspondence, and the story has become about your methodology instead of your finding.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/who-has-the-best-words-a-linguistic-analysis-of-the-democratic-debates (Duolingo blog, 2019-12-19; accessed 2026-09-22)

Duolingo scored named US presidential candidates on the **CEFR A1–C scale** across **6 debates**, using the same internal checker it uses to grade its own content difficulty — and then spent the space to disarm the obvious misreading:

- The score **does not indicate a candidate's intelligence**, and does not describe their command of words off the stage.
- What it actually estimates is **the level at which a second-language learner could be expected to use the word** — a property of the vocabulary, not of the speaker.
- **Intent cannot be inferred.** A simpler word may be a deliberate choice to be widely understood, not a limitation.
- The tool's own resolution is disclosed in the same piece: **C1 and C2 are collapsed into a single "C" tier**, so the top of the scale is coarser than it looks.

Note what this buys. Having said plainly what the score is not, the post can make the interesting claim — how each speaker's word choice shifted debate to debate — without that claim being immediately relitigated.

## The transferable pattern

Write the disclaimer as a design step, not a legal step:

1. **Name the misreading precisely.** Not "results may vary" but "this is not a ranking of X." Generic hedging reads as evasion; a specific denial reads as rigour.
2. **Say what the number is a property of.** The text, the transcript, the log line, the public filing — rarely the human being attached to it.
3. **Say what cannot be inferred.** Intent, effort and skill are almost never recoverable from an output measure, and those are exactly the inferences readers will make.
4. **Disclose the instrument's resolution.** Where it bins, rounds or collapses categories, and therefore where small differences between names are noise.
5. **Put it above the fold, not in a footnote.** The people most likely to misquote you are the ones least likely to scroll.

This costs one paragraph. Skipping it costs the narrative: instead of discussing the finding, you spend the news cycle defending the method, and the correction never travels as far as the original.

## Apply to your product

- If you ranked your customers, partners or competitors on a metric tomorrow, what would a headline writer claim the ranking proves?
- Which property is your score actually a property of — the artifact, the account, or the person — and does your current copy make that unambiguous?
- Where does your metric bin or round, and at what gap between two names is a difference genuinely meaningless?

# Meaning Mutates When a Term Crosses a Boundary

## Concept

When a term moves from the group that coined it into a group that did not, the receiving group does not look up the source meaning. They infer one from the contrast the term appears to be drawing in *their* context. Typically they re-read the opaque part as a **modifier** rather than as the thing itself — and once it is a modifier, it is free to attach to other things. The new sense then propagates faster than anyone notices the original is gone.

The same mechanism governs conventions, not just words: users import the norms of whatever they used before yours and apply them to your product below the level of awareness. When your conventions differ, the mismatch does not read as *different*. It reads as the user being wrong, or your product being rude.

## What Duolingo does

- Duolingo documents the re-scoping pattern through double names: **"chai tea" is tea tea**, "naan bread" is bread bread, "Sahara desert" is desert desert, "Mississippi River" is Great River River (Ojibwe), and "Pendle Hill" is hill-hill hill (Celtic *penn* + Old English *hyll* + modern *hill*). In each case the borrowed head was re-read as a specifier and a native head was added.
- The generalisation step is the dangerous one: Italian *scampi* means shrimp, but English re-read it as the **preparation method** — "that's how scampi got associated with the preparation mode instead of the sea creature" — which is why an English menu can offer chicken scampi. The token detached from its referent entirely and started attaching to new ones. Source: blog.duolingo.com/double-names-chai-tea-naan-bread (Duolingo blog, 2022-11-07; accessed 2026-09-22)
- On imported conventions: Duolingo's linguist describes compulsively using the formal "you" with everyone in Russia and holding people at arm's length without knowing it. Transferring English indirectness ("Could you possibly pass the salt?") into Russian reads as oddly nervous; transferring Russian directness into English reads as extremely rude. Neither speaker can diagnose it from inside, so the result gets attributed to **character**, not to a convention gap — which is why the damage is wildly disproportionate to the size of the difference. Duolingo's response is to model the norms directly in course content: conversation lessons for making requests, explicit formal/informal skills. Source: blog.duolingo.com/how-culture-impacts-language-learning (Duolingo blog, 2021-11-22; accessed 2026-09-22)
- **The tension:** the receiving group's re-reading is not stupid, and it usually wins. "Chai tea" is now simply what that drink is called. You will not roll back a mutated term by being right about its etymology; you can only decide whether to adopt the new sense or spend real effort holding the line.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Treat every term that crosses a team boundary as a fork.** The moment your term appears in another group's documents, two definitions exist. Find out what theirs is before you correct it.
2. **Watch for the modifier re-read specifically.** If people start attaching your term to new heads — "X-style", "the X approach", "X mode" — the token has detached from its referent. That is the point to intervene, because after generalisation it is gone.
3. **Teach the convention at the point of use, not in an onboarding doc.** Conventions transfer unconsciously, so they must be surfaced exactly where the user is about to apply the wrong one. A norms page read on day one does not fire on day forty.
4. **Say "different convention", never "wrong".** Because the mismatch is invisible from inside, both parties default to attributing it to competence or character. Naming the convention gap explicitly is the only thing that converts a personal judgement back into a fixable difference.
5. **Give the crossing an owner.** Terms mutate in the gap between two groups, so someone has to be responsible for that gap — usually whoever wrote the definition. A glossary with no owner records the original sense and nothing about the fork.
6. **Decide deliberately whether to hold or adopt.** Holding a term against drift costs continuous enforcement. Adopting the mutated sense costs one migration and a note in your glossary. Pick, and write down which you picked.

## Apply to your product

- Take one internal term of yours that another team now uses. Ask two people on that team to define it. If the definitions differ from yours, has it already become a modifier for them?
- What conventions does your product assume that the tool your users came from did not — defaults, tone, ordering, what an action implies? Where does that mismatch currently read as your product being rude or broken?
- Where does a term of yours now appear as a modifier — "the X way", "X mode" — in someone else's documents? Is the original referent still attached to it?
- Which of your terms are you still enforcing the "correct" meaning of, and what is that enforcement costing per quarter compared with just adopting the drifted sense?

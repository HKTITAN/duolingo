# Promote Corrections to Their Own Instalment

## Concept

Someone who sends you a correction has read closely enough to find a flaw, cared enough to check it, and spent the effort to tell you. No other audience behavior carries that much intent per message.

The default response — edit the original, say nothing — is the worst available use of it. It teaches the audience that scrutiny is invisible, so scrutiny stops arriving. Publishing the correction as its own follow-up does the opposite: it converts a credibility threat into a second content unit, turns the critic into a contributor, and demonstrates in public that engagement is read.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/words-shared-in-all-languages-suggestions (Duolingo blog, 2023-10-17; accessed 2026-09-22)

- One original post became a **3-part arc**: the original, a second post built entirely from reader-submitted counterexamples, and a third built from reader-submitted new candidates ("I loved researching your creative, thoughtful ideas!").
- The counterexample post is explicitly a response to "a tremendous response" on the original, and credits contributors by name — Tomek and Marta, Sundus, Alice (blog.duolingo.com/words-shared-in-all-languages-exceptions (Duolingo blog, 2023-09-12; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The corrections were substantive enough to force a public reversal, stated in the post's own comic register: "Due to new information, coffee has been forced to withdraw from the competition."
- The corrected claims are quantified rather than hand-waved: **11 exception cases** collected from readers for one word, clustering geographically near Eritrea and Ethiopia; **2 exceptions** for a second word.
- The follow-up is **linked twice from the original** rather than replacing it, so the mistake and its repair both stay visible (blog.duolingo.com/study-tips-from-learners (Duolingo blog, 2023-03-07; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The next instalment was pre-announced with the specific candidates readers had proposed, so contributors had a dated reason to come back.

**Tension.** This works when the error is interesting — a surprising counterexample that makes the topic richer. A shipped factual error that misled people is not a content opportunity, and dressing one up as a fun plot twist reads as evasion. The rule is: promote the correction that adds knowledge, and issue a plain unglamorous fix for the one that caused harm.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Never silently patch.** A quiet edit destroys the only evidence that anyone was reading carefully.
2. **Give the correction its own slot,** with its own headline. A footnote is a demotion; an instalment is an invitation.
3. **Name the people who sent it.** Credit is the payment that produces the next correction.
4. **Link forward from the original and leave the original standing,** so the repair is discoverable by everyone who found the mistake first.
5. **Quantify what changed** — how many cases, which ones, what you now believe. A vague "we've updated this" is not a correction.
6. **State the reversal plainly when you were wrong.** The audience already knows; the only variable is whether you say it first.

## Apply to your product

- What happens today when a customer tells you a published claim of yours is wrong — who sees it, and does anything get published?
- Take the last correction you received. If you had run it as its own post with the sender credited, what would it have cost you, and what would it have bought?
- Which of your corrections add knowledge and which merely repair harm — and do you have two different responses for those two cases?

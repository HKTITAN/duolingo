# One Worked Derivation Beats Ten Rules

## Concept

A list of rules tells the reader what each rule does. It cannot show what happens when they run in sequence — how the output of step one becomes the input of step two, and why the end state is unreachable by any single rule acting alone. That composition is where the intuition lives, and only a full trace exposes it.

The second half of the move is temporal. Explaining a process by pointing backwards leaves it filed as history: something that happened to other people, finished. Running the identical process forward on material the audience is holding right now converts it into something currently happening to them — which is what makes it feel mechanical rather than mysterious.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/language-families-related-languages (Duolingo blog, 2023-10-03; accessed 2026-09-22)

- To explain sound change, Duolingo walks a single word from Latin to Spanish in **4 derivation steps, each with its own named mechanism** rather than a general rule about drift: `omine` (final *-m* dropped) → `omne` (an unstressed syllable next to the stress deleted, compared to how English speakers say "choc'late") → `omre` (two similar sounds, *n* and *r*, dissimilating) → `ombre` (the mouth passing through a *b* in transit from *m* to *r*).
- Each step is legible on its own **and** visibly feeds the next: the deletion in step two is what puts *n* and *r* adjacent so that step three can fire. A list of the four mechanisms would not have shown that.
- The everyday comparison ("choc'late") attaches an unfamiliar mechanism to something the reader already does without noticing.
- It then runs the same process **forward on the present day**: imagine "apartment" becoming the New York word for all homes, "McMansion" the Texas word, "dorm" the International English word — and centuries later no one can tell they shared an ancestor.

**Tension.** One trace is one case. A worked derivation is persuasive out of proportion to its evidential weight, and a reader who has seen exactly one will over-generalize from it — assuming every instance follows the same four steps in the same order. The trace earns intuition, not coverage. Pair it with a statement of how typical the case is, or you have taught a pattern that does not hold.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Pick one real case and run it end to end,** with no steps skipped and nothing summarized as "and so on". The skipped step is usually the one carrying the insight.
2. **Name the mechanism at each step.** "Then it changes" teaches nothing; a named cause per step is what makes the trace reusable.
3. **Show the coupling explicitly** — why this step was only possible because the previous one ran. Composition is the thing a rule list structurally cannot deliver.
4. **Anchor each mechanism to something the audience already does** without thinking about it, so the unfamiliar part is one step away from the familiar.
5. **Then run the process forward on their current situation.** A projection onto material they hold today converts an explanation into a prediction they can watch.
6. **State how representative the case is,** so the trace builds intuition without being mistaken for coverage.

## Apply to your product

- Take the concept your users most often misunderstand. Do you explain it with a rule list, and what would a single end-to-end trace of one real case look like instead?
- Which step in that trace do you normally skip because it is obvious to you — and is it obvious to them?
- What does your process predict about something your audience is holding right now, and would you be willing to publish that prediction?

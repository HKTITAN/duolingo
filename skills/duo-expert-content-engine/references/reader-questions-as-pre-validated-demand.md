# Reader Questions as Pre-Validated Demand

## Concept

A keyword tool reports what people typed into a search box. An inbound question reports that a specific human composed a message, addressed it, and sent it — a cost paid before you ever saw it. That cost is the signal, and no search-volume estimate substitutes for it.

Reproducing the question verbatim does two jobs at once. It guarantees the piece answers a demand that actually exists rather than one you imagined in a planning meeting. And it tells every other person carrying the same question that they were not the only one, which is the part that converts a reader into the next sender.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/what-is-spanish-vosotros (Duolingo blog, 2026-05-26; accessed 2026-09-22)

- "Dear Duolingo" runs as a **weekly** column. Every instalment opens with the reader's question reproduced as an illustrated letter, signed with a pun handle rather than a real name — "Is Vosotros Verb-oten?", "Gone But Not Forgotten", "Know What I Mean", "Opposites Attract", "To Be or Not to Vee", "Non-Binary Plussed", "All Alone".
- Duolingo states it receives **hundreds of learner emails per week**, and posts name volume as the reason a topic was chosen: "we've gotten a number of similar questions about this topic."
- One instalment is built entirely as a quiz assembled from reader questions — the queue supplied not just the topic but the format.
- The format is dense enough that roughly **a dozen posts in a single crawl shard** use it, each one a different named question (blog.duolingo.com/learning-a-dialect (Duolingo blog, 2025-10-14; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- Handoffs between columnists are announced inside the post — "this is my first week taking over the Dear Duolingo column" — so the queue outlives any one author (blog.duolingo.com/dialect-vs-accent-definition (Duolingo blog, 2025-11-25; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**Tension.** The inbox is not a representative sample. It over-weights people engaged enough to write and silent on everyone who left without asking. And running the letter verbatim still leaves you choosing which letters run, so the demand signal is only as honest as your selection rule. A queue-fed column tells you what your engaged users want; it will not tell you why the others went quiet.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Treat the inbound question as the topic list.** Anything a person paid effort to ask has at least one confirmed consumer. A topic you invented has zero until proven otherwise.
2. **Publish the question in the asker's own words.** Their phrasing carries the framing, the confusion, and the terminology you would otherwise have to guess at — and it is what lets a stranger recognize their own problem in it.
3. **Make it recurring, not occasional.** A fixed slot builds return habit and gives the queue somewhere to go. One-off answers do neither.
4. **Anonymize the asker, keep the voice.** A handle preserves the human texture without exposing a customer.
5. **Record why this question and not another.** "We got a lot of these" is a fact about demand worth printing.

## Apply to your product

- Where do your users already ask you things — support tickets, sales calls, community threads, onboarding forms? Which of those queues is currently being answered one-to-one and thrown away?
- If you published the ten questions you answered most often last month verbatim, how many would you be embarrassed by — and is that embarrassment about the question or about your product?
- What is your selection rule for which question runs, and would you be willing to state it in public?

# Invert a Confident Belief With a Primary Source

## Concept

A neutral fact adds to what a reader knows. Inverting a belief they held confidently does something stronger: it updates something they were about to assert out loud, which is why the reaction is sharper and why the piece travels.

The move fails without the artifact. An asserted correction meets an asserted denial, and the exchange ends in preference rather than evidence. Reproducing the primary source — the document, the dated record, the original measurement — forecloses the "no it isn't" response and converts the piece from contrarian to citable. On a contested question there is one more step: name the reason people distort this particular answer, including the reasons you might, before you give it.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/soccer-vs-football (Duolingo blog, 2022-11-17; accessed 2026-09-22)

- "Why Do Americans Call It 'Soccer'?" inverts a belief most readers hold firmly by showing the English coined the word — *Assoc.* clipped to *soccer*, from an 1890s Oxford fad of adding *-er* to shortened words.
- Rather than asserting it, the post reproduces a scan of an actual **14 August 1899** *Daily Telegraph* column praising the new word, sourced to Newspapers.com. The reveal lands as: "And you know what? The English loved 'soccer.'"
- The artifact is what does the work. A reader who wants to argue has to argue with a dated newspaper page, not with Duolingo.
- On a contested question the incentives are pre-registered first. Before answering how different Ukrainian and Russian are, Duolingo warned that "groups could often justify their right to have an independent nation by proving that they had their own unique language — and so sometimes, people who try to minimize the differences between two languages might be attempting to diminish another country's nationhood," then answered plainly (blog.duolingo.com/ukraine-language (Duolingo blog, 2022-03-02; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The same authors mark where the artifact does not exist: one post states that the history of a word is often inconclusive, and that "pie" has been in English for **700 years** with no known source (blog.duolingo.com/history-of-thanksgiving-food-words (Duolingo blog, 2023-11-16; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**Tension.** Reversal is addictive and self-selecting. A program that keeps finding counterintuitive answers is either extraordinarily lucky or is choosing topics by how surprising the conclusion is — which is motivated reasoning with better production values. The check is the artifact requirement: if there is no primary source you can put on the page, you do not have a reversal, you have a take.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Target a belief the audience holds confidently,** not one they merely lack. The energy comes from the update, and there is no update without a prior.
2. **Put the primary artifact on the page.** The dated record, the original document, the raw measurement — reproduced, not described.
3. **Source the artifact so it can be re-pulled.** An unattributed scan is an assertion with a picture attached.
4. **Pre-register the incentives on contested questions,** including the ones that could bias you. Demonstrating you modelled the distortion is what licences the substantive answer.
5. **Refuse the reversal when the artifact is missing.** No source, no inversion — publish the uncertainty instead, which is its own credibility move.
6. **Watch your own hit rate.** If everything you publish overturns something, your topic selection is the story.

## Apply to your product

- What does your market believe confidently about your domain that your own records show is false — and do you hold the document that proves it?
- For your most contested claim, what incentive would a sceptical reader assume is driving you, and have you ever named it in print before answering?
- What are you unable to prove with an artifact, and would you be willing to publish that as its own piece?

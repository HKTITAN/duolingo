# Define the Unit and Re-Cut by Frequency

## Concept

A number detaches from its methodology almost immediately. Once loose, it becomes a free-floating factoid that people round upward for effect, and the exaggerated version is the one that gets remembered. Retroactive correction never catches the original, so publishing the counting rule alongside the count is the only defence available before the fact.

There is a companion habit for numbers you receive rather than publish. When a headline statistic counts the whole inventory, re-cut it by usage frequency before believing it. Inventory counts weight a rarely-touched long tail identically to the items handled a thousand times a day. The two cuts routinely give opposite answers, and most people quoting the statistic mean the frequency answer while citing the inventory one.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/inuit-how-many-words-for-snow (Duolingo blog, 2023-08-09; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo traces the "Inuit words for snow" myth — circulating as **100, 200 and 65** — back to exaggerated readings of late-19th-century fieldwork by Franz Boas, and shows the figure depends entirely on **two undefined choices**: whether to pool dialects (should English count both *trash* and *rubbish*?), and what counts as a single word in a system where one word plus suffixes expresses a whole clause.
- It also separates population-level from individual-level counting — how many terms exist is a different question from how many any one person uses — and lands on **around two dozen** as the honest answer.
- The re-cut is demonstrated on a second statistic. Against the widely-repeated claim that **up to 80% of English words** are borrowed from Latin and French, Cindy Blanco notes "that estimate is really deceptive because it includes every word in the dictionary, even many that the average English speaker rarely — or never — uses," then re-cuts by frequency three ways and gets the opposite answer: **96 of the 100** most common words come from Old English, **88 of 100** core items on the Swadesh list, and **44 of the 100** most common nouns (blog.duolingo.com/history-of-english-language (Duolingo blog, 2025-04-17; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The same discipline is applied to Duolingo's own taxonomy: a survey of language families opens by conceding "how can we know for sure which languages are related? The short answer is: We can't!", naming undocumented cases, lost records, and creoles that "don't fit neatly into the imperfect system we've created" (blog.duolingo.com/language-families-overview (Duolingo blog, 2025-03-04; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**Tension.** Debunking the number risks discarding the real finding underneath it. Duolingo still concedes the underlying claim is directionally true — the vocabulary genuinely is unusually rich, because survival relevance drives it. A correction that leaves the audience believing the opposite of the truth has overshot.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Publish the counting rule in the same breath as the count.** Definition first, number second — because only the number will survive the trip.
2. **Name the choices the count depends on.** Every headline figure hides two or three decisions about what counts as one item; state them or someone else will assume the flattering ones.
3. **Separate population-level from individual-level claims.** How much of a thing exists and how much any one person encounters are different questions with different answers.
4. **Re-cut any inventory statistic by usage frequency before you repeat it.** The catalogue and lived experience routinely disagree, and people mean the second.
5. **Mark the soft boundaries in your own classification.** A taxonomy presented as settled gets applied past its evidence, and the first user it fails discards all of it.
6. **Keep the directional truth when you kill the number.** Correcting the figure is not licence to imply the underlying effect is fake.

## Apply to your product

- Which number does your company repeat most often in public, and could three people on your team independently reproduce it from the same definition?
- Is that number a count of what exists or a count of what gets used — and which one does your audience think you mean?
- Where does your product's own classification scheme have soft edges, and what would it cost to say so in the documentation?

# Solicit Tactics, Credit Them, Add the Mechanism

## Concept

Your instrumentation sees what happens inside your product. It cannot see the notebook beside the laptop, the second app running in parallel, the ritual someone built around a feature you never designed for that purpose. Users discover affordances outside your observability boundary, which makes their submitted tactics genuinely new information rather than engagement filler.

Two additions turn that raw material into an asset. Crediting each contributor by name converts a reader into a participant with a stake in the next instalment. And attaching the mechanism — why the tactic works — converts a list of tricks into something the reader can generalize to a case you did not collect.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/study-tips-from-learners (Duolingo blog, 2023-03-07; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Duolingo invited learners to submit practice tips and published the best ones with **names attached**, each paired with "the learning science behind why they work" — the tactic plus its mechanism, not the tactic alone.
- A screenshot a reader sent was reproduced in the post, which makes the tactic concrete and the contribution visible.
- The same crediting pattern runs through the crowdsourced-counterexamples posts, where individual contributors are named against the specific case they supplied (blog.duolingo.com/words-shared-in-all-languages-exceptions (Duolingo blog, 2023-09-12; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The adjacent move is the specific-outcome testimonial. Duolingo profiled **6 named learners, ages 23 to 71**, from France, Venezuela, Germany and Brazil, each with a checkable achievement rather than praise: a technical architect who presented at a conference in Egypt, a yoga instructor in Madrid who taught her first class in English, a 71-year-old who formed sentences with her American daughter-in-law in Prague. Concrete habits were published too — one practises **twice a day for 15-30 minutes**, one presented **for over 20 minutes** in a company meeting, one **has not missed a day in almost two years** — and so were the nerves and the mistakes (blog.duolingo.com/6-inspiring-stories-from-duolingo-english-learners (Duolingo blog, 2025-03-11; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**Tension.** Publishing what users actually do means publishing things you would not have chosen. Duolingo ran a learner's tip about using ChatGPT for Spanish reading practice — hedged by the learner ("it doesn't come close to Duolingo stories") but still promoting a competing tool inside its own blog. That is the price of the format: the moment you filter submissions down to the ones that flatter you, the audience can tell, and the supply dries up.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Ask your users what they do outside the product.** The workarounds are the highest-value submissions because they are invisible to your instrumentation.
2. **Publish with the contributor's name on their specific contribution.** Generic thanks buys nothing; attribution against a named case buys the next batch.
3. **Attach the mechanism to every tactic.** Without it you have a listicle; with it the reader can invent the variant that fits their situation.
4. **Prefer the checkable outcome to the warm endorsement.** Enthusiasm is unfalsifiable and reads as selection bias; a specific before-and-after lets a stranger map their own situation onto a result.
5. **Include the awkward part** — the fear, the false start, the mistake. It is what makes the story legible as real, and it lets a hesitant reader recognize themselves in the before-state.
6. **Accept that you will publish something off-message.** Filtering to only flattering submissions kills the supply you were building.

## Apply to your product

- What do your users do just outside your product that your analytics cannot see — and have you ever asked them directly?
- Take your last customer story. Does it contain a specific thing that person could not do before and can do now, with a number attached, or only praise?
- If a submitted tactic involved a competitor's tool, would you publish it? What does your answer tell your audience about how filtered the rest of the list is?

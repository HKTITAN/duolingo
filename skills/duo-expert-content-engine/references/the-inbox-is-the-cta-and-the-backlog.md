# The Inbox Is the CTA and the Backlog

## Concept

The hardest part of running a content program is not writing. It is knowing what to write about next, week after week, without the topic list degrading into whatever the team happened to think of on Monday.

The fix is structural: end every instalment with the address people send questions to. That one line does two jobs that are normally owned by different teams. It is the call to action — the thing you ask the reader to do — and it is the intake for next week's editorial backlog. Answering a question in public is what persuades the next person that sending one is worth the effort, so the output of the column is also its input.

## What Duolingo does

Source: blog.duolingo.com/what-is-spanish-vosotros (Duolingo blog, 2026-05-26; accessed 2026-09-22)

- Every "Dear Duolingo" instalment closes with the public submission address, `dearduolingo@duolingo.com`, as the CTA. It is not a newsletter signup, not a product link — it is the intake form for the next post.
- The loop is visibly closed: the post opens with a reader's letter and ends by asking for the next one, so a reader watches the exact mechanism that would publish their own question.
- Cadence is **weekly**, sustained against a stated inflow of **hundreds of learner emails per week** — the queue is deep enough that the slot never goes dark.
- The same closing address appears across roughly a dozen instalments in one crawl shard, each answered under a different credentialed byline (blog.duolingo.com/learning-a-dialect (Duolingo blog, 2025-10-14; accessed 2026-09-22)).
- The intake feeds more than the column. A **3-part series** grew out of one original post, with parts two and three assembled from what readers sent in after reading part one (blog.duolingo.com/words-shared-in-all-languages-suggestions (Duolingo blog, 2023-10-17; accessed 2026-09-22)).

**Tension.** A self-feeding queue optimizes for what the audience already knows to ask about. It will not surface the question nobody has thought to ask, and it quietly biases the program toward the interests of your most engaged segment. A queue-driven column needs a deliberate quota of topics chosen against the queue, or it slowly becomes a mirror.

## The transferable pattern

1. **Make the last line of every instalment the intake address.** Not a signup, not a share button — the channel where the next topic arrives.
2. **Publish the answer where the asker can see it,** because visible fulfilment is the only thing that makes submission look worth the effort.
3. **Treat the queue as inventory, and measure its depth.** A slot you can only fill for three weeks is not a program; it is three posts.
4. **Keep the slot fixed even when a week is thin.** The cadence is what makes people remember the address exists.
5. **Reserve a share of slots for topics the queue would never produce,** so the program can still go where the audience has not yet looked.

## Apply to your product

- What is the last line of your content today, and is it asking for something you can actually use as an input?
- If your queue emptied tomorrow, how many instalments could you publish before the slot goes dark — and what does that number say about whether you have a program or a campaign?
- Which questions would never reach your inbox, and who would you have to go ask to find them?
